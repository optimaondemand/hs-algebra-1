📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a graphical representation of multiplication equations. It visually demonstrates the distributive property. This model is typically a rectangle, where the length and width represent the factors, and the area represents the product. We use algebra tiles to physically solve an equation using this graphical representation.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. An example is \(3x+2\).

Coefficient: The coefficient is the numerical factor in a term of an algebraic expression. For example, in the term \(4x\), the coefficient is \(4\).

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in the polynomial. For example, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+x-9\), the degree is \(3\) since — of all the variables \(x^3\), \(x^2\), and \(x\) — 3 is the largest number.

Distributive Property: The distributive property states that multiplication is distributive over addition or subtraction. In other words, \(c\cdot (b+c)\) equals \(ab+ac\).

Equivalent: In algebra, equivalent expressions have the same value for all values of their variables. For instance, the expressions \(2x+3\) and \(3+2x\) are equivalent because they have the same value for any value of \(x\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For instance, \(6x^2-8x+11\) is an expression.

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number, a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. Terms are the building blocks, so to speak, of expressions. Terms in an expression are separated by addition or subtraction signs. For instance, in the expression \(2x+3\), the terms are \(2x\) and \(3\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to multiply monomials, binomials, and trinomials with degrees 1, 2, or 3. We will explore representing and finding a product using algebra tiles to model the process. Then we will explore two different methods for finding the products of multiplying a monomial by a binomial and trinomial and multiplying a binomial by a binomial and trinomial.

Let's begin by watching a video on multiplying a monomial by a binomial modeled with Algebra tiles. Then multiply a monomial by a binomial using an area model and distribution to see how to work the problem out algebraically.


Now that we have a basic understanding of how to multiply a monomial by a binomial, let's take a look at two different examples using an area model and distribution.

First, we will review an example of multiplying a binomial of degree 2 by a binomial of degree 2 using an area model.


Example 1

Multiply \(5x^2(7x^2-3x)\).

Solution:

For this example, we are using an area model to multiply the monomial \(5x^2\) by the binomial \(7x^2-3x\).

To set up the area model we put the monomial on the side and the two terms from the binomial \(7x^2-3x\) along the top.

Then we multiply what is in the row by what is in the column. So, we begin with multiplying \(5x^2 \cdot 7x^2\) to get the term \(35x^4\).

Then we multiply what is at the beginning of the row with what is in the second column, \(5x^2 \cdot -3x\). This creates the term \(-15x^3\).

As our final step, we write the two terms together as a single expression using their signs as the operators.

The equivalent expression \(35x^4-15x^3\) is our final answer.






Now, let's look at an example of a monomial multiplied by a trinomial using the distributive property.

Example 2

Multiply \(-7x^2(x^2-4x-11)\).

Solution:

In this example, we will distribute the \(-7x^2\) to all the terms inside the ( ) using multiplication.

When we multiply terms, we multiply the coefficients and use the product exponent rule to add the powers of the variables.

So, \(-7x^2\cdot x^2=-7x^4\). We multiply \(-7x^2\) to the remaining two terms remembering to use the rules of multiplying integers. The sign of the term becomes the operator in the final expression.

Once all terms have been multiplied we end up with the equivalent expression \(-7x^4+28x^3+77x^2\) as our final answer.


Try it out!

Multiply \(11x(3x^2-8x+7)\).


Check Yourself

To multiply, we distribute the \(11x\) to each term in the ( ) using multiplication; \((11x\cdot 3x^2)+(11x\cdot -8x)+(11x\cdot 7)\).

The answer is \(33x^3-88x^2+77x\).

What do you notice about each term in the final answer compared to the original polynomial in the ( )? Each term is divisible by 11 and each term has at least an in it. This would be considered a common factor in each term, which is the same as the monomial we originally distributed. Seeing this common factor in the answer will be important when we explore factoring in Module 6.

Here is one more example of multiplying two binomials using both methods. Remember, that for each example we have worked through so far, either method can be used. When you work them out yourself, you can choose either method!

Example 3

Multiply \((-3x+6)(5x-8)\).

Solution:

One key thing to remember when multiplying two binomials using either method, you will most likely have a pair of like terms that need to be combined using knowledge gained in the previous lesson. In order to have the most simplified complete expression in the end, you will want to be sure to combine any like terms.

In the example shown here the \(24x\) and \(30x\) need to be combined. Since they are both positive, we will add them to get the term \(54x\).

The last two lines for each solution method look identical, as they should. Whichever method you choose to use, you will end up with the same answer, provided you multiply and combine terms correctly.

For this example, the equivalent expression \(-15x^2+54x-48\) is our final answer.






Try it out!

Multiply.

1. \((7x-2)(x+9)\)

2. \((x+8)(x+6)\)


Check yourself

The answers are:

1. \(7x^2-61x-18\)

2. \(x^2+14x+48\)

Take a look at the last term and the coefficient of the middle term of the second answer, and compare those numbers to the constants in the original problem. You may notice that the coefficient of the middle term (14) is the sum of the two numbers (8 and 6) and the last term (48) is their product. This pattern will be important when we explore factoring quadratics in Module 6. Here is a simple formula to keep in mind when multiplying two binomials that have x terms with no coefficient: if \((x+a)(x+b)\), then \(x^2+(a+b)x+(a\cdot b)\). This works if \(a\) or \(b\) or both are being subtracted. In that case just use the subtraction sign as a negative when applying the formula: if \((x-a)(x-b)\), then \(x^2+(-a-b)x+(-a\cdot -b)\) or \(x^2-(a+b)x+(ab)\). Note that in the case of a minus sign in the original parenthetical terms, the middle term of the solution will be negated but the final term will be positive (since a negative number times a negative number is positive).

 

Next, we will look at two special cases of multiplying two binomials.

First special case.

Example 4

Multiply \((3x-5)^2\).

Solution:

Remember when something is squared, it means to multiply it by itself. So, \((3x-5)^2=(3x-5)(3x-5)\). To find the product we will multiply \((3x–5)\) by itself.

To multiply in this example, we used the area model.

Notice that the two middle terms are –15x that, when combined, make the term \(-30x\).

You may also notice that the first term of the answer is the square of the first term of the ( ). The last term of the answer also is the square of the last term of the ( ).

An easier way to look at this problem may be as follows.

The first term is \((3x)^2=9x^2\).

The second term is \(2(3x\cdot -5)=2(-15x)=-30x\)

The last term is \((-5)^2=25\).

This gives the final answer of \(9x^2-30x+25\).

This will be true whenever you are squaring a binomial, whether the operator is a + or a – sign. The formula is \((ax+b)^2=(ax)^2+2(a\cdot b)x+(b)^2\).

 

Try it out!

1. Find the product: \((4x+7)^2\)

2. Find the product: \((7y^2-2)^2\) 

 

Check Yourself

1. The answer is \(16x^2+56x+49\)

2. The answer is \(49y^4-28y^2+4\)

 


Let's look at the next special case.

Example 5

Multiply \((3x-4)(3x+4)\).

Solution:

You may first notice that the coefficients and constants are the same number. The only difference between the ( ) is one is subtraction and one is addition. Let's use distribution and see what the final answer to this one is. Watch for any patterns you might notice when multiplying and the final answer.

You may have noticed, that similar to the last example we looked at, the first term of the final answer is the first term of the ( )'s squared. Because the terms are the same we are basically squaring the term.

The second and third terms come out as opposites of the same term, one positive and one negative. Thus, they zero each other making no middle term in the final answer.

Then the last term is the opposite or negative of \(4^2\) because there is a positive and a negative of the same number.

Therefore, we can think of \((ax+b)(ax-b)\) as \((ax)^2-(b)^2\). The middle terms will also zero each other out since there will be a positive and negative of the same term.





Try it out!

Find the product. \((5x+9)(5x-9)\)


Check Yourself

The answer is \(25x^2-81\).

Here is the last example for this lesson.

Example 6

Multiply \((-4x+7)(x^2-9x+5)\).

Solution:

The biggest thing to remember when working on problems like this is that both terms in the first ( ) must be multiplied by all three terms in the second ( ). The organization of the area model can help assist in ensuring everything gets multiplied by being able to see that each cell is filled with a term representing the product of the terms in that row and column.

If you choose to use the distributive property, drawing the arrows as you multiply each term in the second ( ) by each term in the first ( ) can help in making sure not to miss any products. This was demonstrated in the video you watched.

You may also notice that there were two sets of like terms that needed to be combined in this example when generating the final expression. This will most likely always be the case when multiplying a binomial by a trinomial.

For this example, the equivalent expression \(-4x^3+43x^2-83x+35\) is the final answer.
