📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a visual tool often used to simplify multiplication problems, particularly those involving multi-digit numbers or algebraic expressions. This model is typically a rectangle, with the length and width representing the factors, and the area representing the product.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. For example, \(3x+2\) is a binomial.

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in that polynomial. For instance, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the degree is \(3\) because the highest power of the variable \(x\) is \(3\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For example, \(2x^2+3x-2\) is an expression.

Factoring: Factoring is the process of breaking down a number, an expression, or a polynomial into its smallest components. The factors of the polynomial \(x^2-4\) are \((x-2)\) and \((x+2)\).

Factor by Grouping: Factoring by grouping is a method used to factor polynomials with four or more terms. The terms are grouped in pairs in such a way that each group has a common factor. The common factors are then factored out of each group, and if the expression is factorable, a common binomial factor will appear.

Greatest Common Factor (GCF): The Greatest Common Factor between two or more numbers or algebraic terms is the largest number or term that divides exactly into each of the numbers or terms. For instance, the GCF of the monomials \(12x^3\) and \(18x^2\) is \(6x^2\).

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Polynomial: A polynomial is a type of mathematical expression that can have constants, variables, and exponents, which can be combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. It's important to note that in a polynomial, variables' exponents must be non-negative integers. Examples include \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\).

Standard Form of a Polynomial: The standard form of a polynomial arranges its terms by degree in descending order. The term with the highest degree is written first, followed by the term with the next highest degree, and so on until the term with the lowest degree (which is often the constant term). For example, the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\) is in standard form.

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number (also known as a constant), a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. In the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the terms are \(2x^3\), \(-5x^2\), \(4x\), and \(-7\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will be exploring how to factor out a greatest common factor (GCF) from a polynomial with 2 or 3 terms, with terms having one or two variables. We will also be exploring how to factor a polynomial with 4 terms using grouping.

Let's begin with exploring how to factor out a GCF from a polynomial. In a previous lesson we learned how to multiply a GCF to a polynomial, we used an area model to help us multiply. We multiplied the GCF in the row header to each term of the polynomial in the column headers.

To factor out a GCF is like undoing the distribution of a GCF. We will use an area model in reverse to see how to do this. Watch the video to see how we find and factor out a GCF from a given polynomial.

Now, we will look at two more examples using an area model and dividing by a monomial techniques to factor out a GCF from a given polynomial.


Example 1

Factor out the GCF from \(8x^3-24x^2+32x\).

Solution:

The first thing you want to do is identify what each term has in common with each other. Sometimes this can be a number, sometimes a variable, and sometimes both. For this polynomial, I notice that each term has the factor 8 in the coefficients and each term has at least 1 x. So, my GCF is \(8x\). Now, we will use an area model to factor out the GCF.

When we are given the polynomial and asked to factor out the GCF, that given polynomial is the answer part of the area model. So, we fill in each term into the cells below each column heading. So, we write in each term \(8x^3\), \(-24x^2\), and \(32x\).

Next, we determine what the GCF is and write it into the row header. In this polynomial, we determined the GCF was . So, that was filled in for the row header.

Area models are used to multiply the terms in the row header(s) with the terms in the column header(s). Since we are trying to find the column headers, we will use the inverse of multiplying which is dividing. So, now we will divide each term in the answer polynomial (in purple) by the GCF in the row header (in green).

When we divide , we get \(x^2\). Next, divide and get \(-3x\). Finally, divide and get \(4\).

When we write the final answer, we put the GCF we factored out on the outside of a ( ) and we put the factored polynomial on the inside of the ( ).

So, our final answer is \(8x(x^2-3x+4)\). You can check this answer, by distributing back using multiplication the \(8x\) to the polynomial in the ( ) and see if you get the original polynomial given.


Now, let's look at an example just using division.

Example 2

Factor out the GCF from \(63x^4+14x^3-21x^2\).

Solution:

First, let's determine what the GCF is. I notice that each term has a factor of 7 in its coefficient. I also notice that each term has at least an \(x^2\). Therefore, my GCF is \(7x^2\). Now, let's divide each term by that GCF.

You will notice in this one, we rewrote the original polynomial and divide each term in it by the GCF \(7x^2\).

For the answer, we wrote the GCF on the outside of a ( ) and as we divided each term, we wrote the new term inside the ( ) going in order.

So, the final answer is \(7x^2(9x^2+2x-3)\). Again, you can check your answer by multiplying each term inside the ( ) by the GCF \(7x^2\). You should get the original polynomial.

Example 3

Factor \(6x^3-14x^2-9x+21\).

Solution:

One of the things you may notice about polynomials that can be factored using the factor-by-grouping method is that they generally have 4 terms where you can make 2 groups of 2 terms to factor out a GCF from. The most important thing to remember is when you factor out the GCF from each group, the binomial that remains in each group must be the same to continue using the method. If they are not the same, then either the full GCF was not factored out or the polynomial is not factorable by this method.

To factor this polynomial using factor-by-grouping, we began with grouping together the first two terms \((6x^3-14x^2)\). Then we grouped together the last two terms \((-9x+21)\).

Notice that when the last two terms were grouped together, we kept the minus sign as a negative on the 9x and put a + between the two groups. You always want to be sure to include - signs inside the group and only use a + sign between the groups.

Next, we see the GCF of the first group is \(2x^2\). So, we factor out the GCF creating the term \(2x^2(3x-7)\).

For the second ( ), we notice the GCF is \(-3\). After we factor out that GCF, we have \(-3(3x-7)\).

Once the GCF's have been factored out of both groups, we will see that what remains in both groups is the same, \((3x-7)\). So, now we can pull that whole group out, leaving us with the two GCF's in a group. This creates the final answer \((2x^3-3)(3x-7)\). This can also be written in reverse order as \((3x-7)(2x^3-3)\) and is also correct. What is important for the answer, is what is in each ( ).
