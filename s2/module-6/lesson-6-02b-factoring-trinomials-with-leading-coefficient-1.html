📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a visual tool often used to simplify multiplication problems, particularly those involving multi-digit numbers or algebraic expressions. This model is typically a rectangle, with the length and width representing the factors, and the area representing the product.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. For example, \(3x+2\) is a binomial.

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in that polynomial. For instance, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the degree is \(3\) because the highest power of the variable \(x\) is \(3\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For example, \(2x^2+3x-2\) is an expression.

Factoring: Factoring is the process of breaking down a number, an expression, or a polynomial into its smallest components. The factors of the polynomial \(x^2-4\) are \((x-2)\) and \((x+2)\).

Leading Coefficient: The leading coefficient is the numerical factor of the term in a polynomial that contains the highest power of the variable. For instance, in the polynomial \(-3x^4+5x^2-2\), the leading coefficient is \(-3\).

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Polynomial: A polynomial is a type of mathematical expression that can have constants, variables, and exponents, which can be combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. It's important to note that in a polynomial, variables' exponents must be non-negative integers. Examples include \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\).

Standard Form of a Polynomial: The standard form of a polynomial arranges its terms by degree in descending order. The term with the highest degree is written first, followed by the term with the next highest degree, and so on until the term with the lowest degree (which is often the constant term). For example, the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\) is in standard form.

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number (also known as a constant), a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. In the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the terms are \(2x^3\), \(5x^2\), \(4x\), and \(-7\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will be exploring how to factor quadratic trinomials with a leading coefficient of 1 using algebra tiles, an area model, and other factoring techniques. We will explore patterns about the constants in the binomials when factored compared to the value of the coefficient of the x term and the value of the constant in the original trinomial that was factored in order to develop factoring rules for quadratic trinomials.

We will also explore factoring another trinomial using an area model and grouping to make connections and explore the structure of the binomials created.

Now that you have seen an example of factoring a quadratic trinomial, let's review a couple of examples using the different methods and make connections between the methods to develop a rule for factoring a trinomial in the form \(x^2+bx+c\).


The first example we will look at we will use an area model to factor. Notice how we find the factor pairs of the constant in the trinomial and find the sums of each of those pairs to find the pair that makes the coefficient of the middle x term.

Example 1

Factor \(x^2-4x-21\).

Solution:

In the work for this example, we began with finding and listing out the factor pairs for the constant, \(-21\). We ended up with four factor pairs because \(-1\cdot 21\) creates a different sum than \(1\cdot -21\).

Once we had all the combinations of the factor pairs listed, we then found the sum of each pair.

\(-1+21=20\)
\(1+-21=-20\)
\(-3+7=4\)
\(3+-7=-4\)

Notice the last pair of factors, when added together creates the \(-4\) coefficient on the x term in the trinomial. This means that we will use \(3\) and \(-7\) as coefficients of x to split the middle term \(-4x\) into \(3x + -7x\).

So, now we can fill in the four cells of our area model and use GCFs to find the row and column headings to create the binomials.

  • First, we fill in the \(x^2\) term in the top left corner of the 4 middle cells.
  • Next, we fill in the newly created terms \(3x\) and \(-7x\) into the top right and bottom left cells. It does not matter which one goes where. If reversed, then the binomials will be reversed, but it will still be correct.
  • Last, we fill in the \(-21\) in the bottom right cell.

Now, we look at each row and each column and identify the GCF that we write in the row or column heading.

  • For example, when we look at the row containing \(x^2\) and \(-7x\), we can identify \(x\) as the GCF. So we write in an \(x\) in the row header to the left of that row.
  • We continue this process with the bottom row and each of the columns.
  • This is how all the terms \(x\), \(-7\), \(x\), and \(3\) are found.
  • For the final answer, we take the two terms on the top row (the column headers) and create the binomial \((x-7)\).
  • Then we take the two terms in the very first column (the row headers) and create the binomial \((x+3)\).

The final answer for the factored form of the trinomial \(x^2-4x-21\) is \((x-7)(x+3)\). If the binomials are reversed and written as \((x+3)(x-7)\), this would also be a correct answer.


Next, let's explore factoring using the factor-by-grouping method after we split the middle term.

Example 2

Factor \(x^2-11x+18\).

Solution:

For this example, we began by listing out the factor pairs for 18. Keep in mind that even though 18 is positive, you can use two negative factors since a negative times a negative makes a positive.

We noticed in our factor pairs that if we add 2 and 9 it makes 11. However, we need to make -11.

Instead of using \(2\cdot 9=18\), we can use \(-2\cdot -9=18\). This allows us to find the sum \(-2+-9=-11\) which is equal to the coefficient of our middle term.

So, we can rewrite the trinomial by splitting the \(-11x\) to generate the expression \(x^2-2x+-9x+18\). This allows us to group the first two terms and group the last two terms and use the factor-by-grouping method we learned about in the previous lesson.

For the first group, we notice a GCF of \(x\). So, we can factor out the \(x\) generating the expression \(x(x-2)\).

In the second group, we notice the GCF of \(-9\). So, we can factor out the \(-9\) to generate the expression \(-9(x-2)\).

Since the two groups are now identical, we can pull those out as one and create the other group \((x-9)\). This gives us the expression \((x-9)(x-2)\) as our final answer.

Hopefully, you are beginning to see a pattern in the structure of the two binomials of the factored trinomial. The two numbers that go into the binomials are equal to the pair of numbers we find that will multiply to be the constant of the trinomial and add to be the coefficient of the middle x term. The first term in the binomials is x for both which comes from the \(x^2\) term since \(x\cdot x=x^2\).


Let's explore one last example, using the factoring rule discussed above.

Example 3

Factor \(x^2+6x-16\).

Solution:

For this example and like all other examples, we began by listing the factor pairs of \(-16\).

Once we had them all listed, we looked for the factor pair that when added would equal \(6\).

We found that \(-2\cdot 8=-16\) and \(-2+8=6\). Thus, the numbers \(-2\) and \(8\) are the pair of numbers that generated this trinomial.

If needed, you can use one of the previous methods. Otherwise, you might have now realized that these two numbers are the numbers used in the binomials for the factored form.

Therefore, \(x^2+6x-16\) in factored form is \((x-2)(x+8)\).
