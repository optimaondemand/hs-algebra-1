📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a visual tool often used to simplify multiplication problems, particularly those involving multi-digit numbers or algebraic expressions. This model is typically a rectangle, with the length and width representing the factors, and the area representing the product.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. For example, \(3x+2\) is a binomial.

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in that polynomial. For instance, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the degree is \(3\) because the highest power of the variable \(x\) is \(3\).

Difference of Two Squares: This is a term used to describe expressions of the form \(a^2-b^2\), where \(a\) and \(b\) can be any real numbers, variables, or expressions. The special thing about this form is that it can be factored into \((a+b)(a-b)\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For example, \(2x^2+3x-2\) is an expression.

Factoring: Factoring is the process of breaking down a number, an expression, or a polynomial into its smallest components. The factors of the polynomial \(x^2-4\) are \((x-2)\) and \((x+2)\).

Leading Coefficient: The leading coefficient is the numerical factor of the term in a polynomial that contains the highest power of the variable. For instance, in the polynomial \(3x^4+5x^2-2\), the leading coefficient is \(-3\).

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Perfect Square: A perfect square is a number that can be expressed as the square of an integer. For example, 1, 4, 9, and 16 are perfect squares, as they can be written as \(1^2\), \(2^2\), \(3^2\), and \(4^2\), respectively.

Perfect Square Trinomial: A perfect square trinomial is a special form of trinomial (a polynomial with three terms) that can be factored into the square of a binomial. In other words, it's a trinomial that can be written in the form \((a+b)^2\) or \((a-b)^2\). For example, the trinomials \(x^2+2xy+y^2\) and \(x^2-2xy+y^2\) are perfect square trinomials because they can be factored to \((x+y)^2\) and \((x-y)^2\), respectively.

Polynomial: A polynomial is a type of mathematical expression that can have constants, variables, and exponents, which can be combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. It's important to note that in a polynomial, variables' exponents must be non-negative integers. Examples include \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\).

Standard Form of a Polynomial: The standard form of a polynomial arranges its terms by degree in descending order. The term with the highest degree is written first, followed by the term with the next highest degree, and so on until the term with the lowest degree (which is often the constant term). For example, the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\) is in standard form.

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number (also known as a constant), a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. In the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the terms are \(2x^3\), \(-5x^2\), \(4x\), and \(-7\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

Now let's look at an example of factoring out a GCF and then factoring a trinomial using the factoring rule developed in lesson 3.02b.

Example 1

Factor completely \(4x^3-24x^2-28x\).

Solution:

First, we will notice that all three terms have the GCF \(4x\) in them. So, we begin by factoring out the GCF \(4x\).

This creates the expression \(4x(x^2-6x-7)\).

Next, we notice that we are left with a trinomial. We will look at the factor pairs of \(-7\) that make a sum of \(-6\). We find that \(-7\cdot 1=-7\) and \(-7+1=-6\).

So, we can use the rule to factor the trinomial \(x^2-6x-7\) into the binomials \((x-7)(x+1)\).

Keeping the GCF we took out at the beginning with the two binomial factors we just found, we can create the factored expression \(4x(x-7)(x+1)\).








Next, we will look at an example where we take out a GCF and then need to factor a trinomial with a leading coefficient. These can get messy if you use factor-by-grouping method. It can be helpful to use [ ] when you take out the GCF so as not to confuse it with the ( ) when you use grouping.

Example 2

Factor completely \(24x^3-10x^2-4x\).

Solution:

In this example, we first notice that the trinomial has the term \(2x\) in common in all the terms. So, we begin with factoring out the 2x.

We are left with a trinomial in the form \(ax^2+bx+c\). We need to multiply the leading coefficient and constant together, \(12\) and \(-2\), to get \(-24\). We find factor pairs of \(-24\) that will create a sum of \(-5\). We find that the factor pair \(-8\) and \(3\) works.

So, we use factor-by-grouping method to split the middle term \(-5x\) into \(-8x\) and \(3x\). Then we group the first two terms and the last two terms. We take out the GCF in each group. Notice that in the second group the only GCF is 1. So, we put a +1 on the outside to get two ( ) that are identical we can factor out and leave us with two terms for the other binomial factor.

The final answer for the completely factored polynomial is \(2x(4x+1)(3x-2)\).

Now we will look at an example that leaves us with a difference of two squares when we factor out a GCF.

Example 3

Factor completely \(7x^3y-63xy^3\).

Solution:

In this example, we notice there was a GCF of \(7xy\) that we factored out first.

This left us with a difference of squares to factor.

To factor the difference of squares we note that \(x^2=x\cdot x\), and that \(-9y^2=-3y\cdot 3y\).

So the difference of squares factors into \((x-3y)(x+3y)\).

The final factored form of the original polynomial is \(7xy(x-3y)(x+3y)\).






Last, we will take a look at factoring out a GCF and then using grouping.

Example 4

Factor completely \(6x^4-12x^3+18x^2-36x\).

Solution:

In this example, once we factor out the GCF \(6x\) we are left with \(4\) terms in the polynomial. We will use the factor-by-grouping method to factor the polynomial further.

Notice that we are left with a binomial with a squared term in the final factored binomials. This binomial is a sum and 3 is not a perfect square.

However, we do want to check any binomials to ensure there are no difference of squares that could be factored further.

So, the final factored form for this polynomial is \(6x(x^2+3)(x-2)\).

❓ ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

1. Explain and demonstrate the factor-by-grouping method. How does this method help in factoring a polynomial?

2. What is a monomial, and how can you factor a polynomial by removing a common monomial?

3. How would factoring a trinomial in the form \(x^2+bx-c\) be different or the same as factoring one in the form \(x^2-bx-c\)? What do you need to consider for the value of \(b\) and \(c\) when factoring?

4. What are the methods you can use to rewrite a polynomial expression as a product of polynomials?

5. How is factoring a trinomial with a leading coefficient not equal to 1 different and/or the same as factoring a trinomial with a leading coefficient of 1?

6. How does factoring work in the context of perfect square trinomials and difference of squares?

7. How do these factoring techniques apply to real-world problems? Can you think of a real-world scenario where these techniques could be useful?
