📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a visual tool often used to simplify multiplication problems, particularly those involving multi-digit numbers or algebraic expressions. This model is typically a rectangle, with the length and width representing the factors, and the area representing the product.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. For example, \(3x+2\) is a binomial.

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in that polynomial. For instance, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the degree is \(3\) because the highest power of the variable \(x\) is \(3\).

Difference of Two Squares: This is a term used to describe expressions of the form \(a^2-b^2\), where \(a\) and \(b\) can be any real numbers, variables, or expressions. The special thing about this form is that it can be factored into \((a+b)(a-b)\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For example, \(2x^2+3x-2\) is an expression.

Factoring: Factoring is the process of breaking down a number, an expression, or a polynomial into its smallest components. The factors of the polynomial \(x^2-4\) are \((x-2)\) and \((x+2)\).

Leading Coefficient: The leading coefficient is the numerical factor of the term in a polynomial that contains the highest power of the variable. For instance, in the polynomial \(-3x^4+5x^2-2\), the leading coefficient is \(-3\).

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Perfect Square: A perfect square is a number that can be expressed as the square of an integer. For example, 1, 4, 9, and 16 are perfect squares, as they can be written as \(1^2\), \(2^2\), \(3^2\), and \(4^2\), respectively.

Perfect Square Trinomial: A perfect square trinomial is a special form of trinomial (a polynomial with three terms) that can be factored into the square of a binomial. In other words, it's a trinomial that can be written in the form \((a+b)^2\) or \((a-b)^2\). For example, the trinomials \(x^2+2xy+y^2\) and \(x^2-2xy+y^2\) are perfect square trinomials because they can be factored to \((x+y)^2\) and \((x-y)^2\), respectively.

Polynomial: A polynomial is a type of mathematical expression that can have constants, variables, and exponents, which can be combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. It's important to note that in a polynomial, variables' exponents must be non-negative integers. Examples include \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\).

Standard Form of a Polynomial: The standard form of a polynomial arranges its terms by degree in descending order. The term with the highest degree is written first, followed by the term with the next highest degree, and so on until the term with the lowest degree (which is often the constant term). For example, the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\) is in standard form.

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number (also known as a constant), a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. In the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+4x-7\), the terms are \(2x^3\), \(-5x^2\), \(4x\), and \(-7\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

Now that you have seen how to factor a few perfect square trinomials using an area model and the factor-by-grouping method, let's review with a few examples and develop a rule for factoring perfect square trinomials with and without a leading coefficient.


Let's begin by looking at an example with a leading coefficient of 1.

Example 1

Factor \(x^2+16x+64\).

Solution:

In this example, we may notice that \(16x\) can be found using \(8x+8x\) and \(64\) can be found by \(8\cdot 8\). Using the pattern we reviewed in the prior knowledge check, we can rewrite the trinomial as follows \((x)^2+(2)(8)(x)+(8)^2\).

This allows us to see that we can write it as the product of the two binomials \((x+8)(x+8)\) or \((x+8)^2\).

Using factor-by-grouping, we can rewrite the original trinomial as \(x^2+8x+8x+64 \implies (x^2+8x)+(8x+64)\). Then we can look for the GCF in each grouping.

In the first group, the GCF is x and can be rewritten as \(x(x+8)\). In the second group, the GCF is \(8\) and can be rewritten as \(8(x+8)\).

Now, that the two groups are identical, we can factor out the group and rewrite it as \((x+8)(x+8)\) or \((x+8)^2\).




Now, let's look at an example with a leading coefficient greater than 1.

Example 2

Factor \(16x^2-40x+25\).

Solution:

In the work shown for the example, we used the rule that the perfect square trinomial is in the form \((ax)^2+2(a\cdot b)x+(b)^2\), which factors as \((ax+b)^2\).

We noticed that \((4x)^2=16x^2\), making \(ax=4x\).

We also notice that \((-5)^2=25\), making \(b=-5\).

We also know that \(2\cdot (4x)\cdot (-5)=-40x\) which is the middle term. So the trinomial follows the pattern of a perfect square trinomial and can be factored into the form \((ax-b)^2\).

Therefore, the factored form of \(16x^2-40x+25\) is \((4x-5)^2\).

Now that we have seen how to factor a difference of squares binomial, let's review by looking at two examples using the rule for factoring.

Example 3

Factor \(x^2-49\).

Solution:

We can rewrite the binomial as a trinomial using 0x as the middle term.

The factors of the constant \(-49\) are \(7\) and \(-7\).

We know that \(-7x+7x = 0x\).

Therefore, the binomial can be factored as \((x+7)(x-7)\), using the factors \(-7\) and \(7\).






Let's look at one more example of factoring a difference of squares binomial with a leading coefficient.

Example 4

Factor \(36x^2-25\).

Solution:

In the work for this example, we worked out using the rule and the factor-by-grouping method to compare.

If a binomial is in the form \(a^2-b^2\), then it can be factored as \((a+b)(a-b)\).

We notice that the first term \(36x^2\) can be rewritten as \((6x)^2\) and the second term \(-25\) is \(-5\cdot 5\).

So, the binomial can be factored as follows \((6x+5)(6x-5)\).


The factor-by-grouping method uses the terms \(-30x\) and \(30x\) to create the middle \(0x\) term. Using GCF's for each group, we end up with the same factors as using the rule.

For our last example, we will look at a quartic, 4th-degree polynomial, that is in the form of a difference of two squares.

Example 5

Factor \(x^4-81\).

Solution:

We start with a look at how we can rewrite the term \(x^4\) as a term squared. We notice that \(x^2\cdot x^2=x^4\) using exponent properties from the first module. So, \(x^4\) can be rewritten as \((x^2)^2\), where \(a=x^2\).

We notice that \(81\) is equivalent to \(-9\cdot 9\) and can be rewritten as \(-9^2\), where \(b=9\).

Following the rule for factoring, the binomial factors as \((x^2+9)(x^2-9)\).

Then, we will notice that the second binomial factor or the factor \((x^2-9)\) is another difference of squares that can be factored again into \((x+3)(x-3)\) using the factoring rule.

The second binomial \((x^2+9)\) is not a difference of squares since the constant is being added and not subtracted, it does not follow form.

Thus, the final factored form of \(x^4-81\) is \((x+3)(x-3)(x^2+9)\).
