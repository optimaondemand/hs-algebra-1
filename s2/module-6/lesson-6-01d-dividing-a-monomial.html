📚 VOCABULARY

Area Model: An area model is a graphical representation of multiplication equations. It visually demonstrates the distributive property. This model is typically a rectangle, where the length and width represent the factors, and the area represents the product.

Binomial: A binomial is an algebraic expression that has exactly two terms. These terms are separated by either a plus or minus sign. An example is \(3x+2\).

Coefficient: The coefficient is the numerical factor in the term of an algebraic expression. For example, in the term \(4x\), the coefficient is 4.

Degree: The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable in the polynomial. For example, in the polynomial \(2x^3-5x^2+x-9\), the degree is \(3\).

Distributive Property: The distributive property states that multiplication is distributive over addition or subtraction. In other words, \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\).

Dividend: The dividend is the number that you are dividing. In the equation \(12\div 3=4\), the number \(12\) is the dividend.

Divisor: The divisor is the number you are dividing by in a division operation. In the same division equation \(12\div 3=4\), the number \(3\) is the divisor.

Equivalent: In algebra, equivalent expressions have the same value for all values of their variables. For instance, the expressions \(2x+3\) and \(3+2x\) are equivalent because they have the same value for any value of \(x\).

Expression: An expression is a mathematical phrase that can include numbers, variables, operation symbols, and grouping symbols. For instance, \(6x^2-8x+11\) is an expression.

Monomial: A monomial is an algebraic expression with only one term. It can be a single number (like 5), a variable (like x), or a number multiplied by a variable (like 5x).

Quotient: The quotient is the result you get when you divide one number by another. In the division equation \(12\div 3=4\), the quotient is \(4\).

Term: A term in algebra can be a single number, a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. Terms in an expression are separated by addition or subtraction signs. For instance, in the expression \(2x+3\), the terms are \(2x\) and \(3\).

Trinomial: A trinomial is an algebraic expression with three terms. For example, \(x^2+5x+4\) is a trinomial.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will be exploring dividing a polynomial by a monomial. In the previous lesson, we learned about multiplying polynomials by a monomial. We will be using that knowledge to help us understand how to divide that monomial back out of a polynomial.

When we learned about multiplying a polynomial by a monomial, you may have noticed that when you multiply two variables, the exponent or degree of the variable increases. For example, \(x^2 \cdot x^3=x^5\). This is due to the product of a power property that states to add the exponents when expressions with the same base are multiplied.

Thus, when you divide a polynomial by a monomial, the degree of the polynomial and the exponent of each term will decrease. The quotient of a power property states to subtract the exponents when the same bases are divided.

When we were multiplying polynomials, we explored two methods. We first explored multiplying using an area model. Then we explored multiplying using the distributive property by multiplying.

Since division is the inverse operation of multiplication, we can use similar methods to divide a polynomial by a monomial.

In this example, we are using the distributive property and quotient of a power property by subtracting the exponents of the variables.

We begin by rewriting the expression using the distributive property to show that each term in the dividend is divided by the divisor.

As we divide each term by the divisor, we divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents of each variable by the exponent of the corresponding variable in the divisor.

For the first term, we will divide \(24\) and \(4\) to get a coefficient of \(6\). Then subtract the exponents on \(x\), \(7-2=5\). Then subtract the exponents of \(y\), \(5-1=4\). Remember if a variable does not have an exponent, it is an understood 1. These steps create the first term of the final expression \(6x^5y^4\). We continue with the next two terms, dividing the coefficients, subtracting the exponents on x, and subtracting the exponents on y.

After we complete all those steps, we end up with the equivalent expression \(6x^5y^4-4x^3y^3+2x\) as our final answer that represents the quotient.

Example 4

Find the quotient.

\((10x^3-14x^2+6x-5)\div (2x)\)

Solution:

Remember, when using an area model you divide each term in the row by the term at the beginning of the row. An area model can be used to multiply each column by each row. So, when used in a division problem, we are doing the inverse.

For the last term, you see we are dividing \(-5\) by \(2x\). This creates the term \(-\frac{5}{2x}\). You will notice in the long division method, the \(-5\) is a remainder as \(2x\) cannot divide into \(-5\).

The area model helps to see how to write remainders as a term that becomes part of the final expression. In the long division method, one will need to remember to write any remainders as a term divided by the divisor.

In this example, the equivalent expression \(5x^2-7x+3-\frac{5}{2x}\) represents the quotient and is the final answer.

One thing to note is the degree of the original polynomial that was divided and the degree of the final answer. In each term of the final answer, the exponent is one less than the original polynomial—the degree of the original polynomial was 3 and the degree of the quotient is 2 since the degree of the divisor was 1 (3 – 1 = 2).
