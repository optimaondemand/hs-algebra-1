📚 VOCABULARY

Association: A relationship or pattern observed between two categorical variables in a data set.

Bivariate Categorical Data: Data that consists of two variables, each sorted into categories, and used to study relationships between them.

Column Marginal: The total frequency for each column in a two-way frequency table.

Frequency: The number of times a particular category or combination appears in a data set.

Joint Frequencies: The values inside the table that represent the frequency for a specific pair of category values (one from each variable).

Marginal Frequencies: The totals found at the edges (margins) of the table, representing the total for each row or each column.

Percentages: A number expressed as a part of 100, used to show how large one quantity is compared to another.

Population: The entire group of individuals or items being studied in a survey or experiment.

Probability: A value between 0 and 1 that represents the likelihood of an event occurring.

Relative Frequency: A frequency expressed as a fraction or decimal of the total number of data points.

Row Marginal: The total frequency for each row in a two-way table.

Total Frequency: The sum of all frequencies in a two-way table; the number in the bottom-right corner.

Two-Way Frequency Table: A table used to display the frequency counts of two categorical variables and to analyze relationships between them.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to interpret and calculate joint and marginal relative frequencies from a completed two-way frequency table. Previously, we learned how to construct a two-way frequency table using categorical data. Now we take it one step further by using the information in those tables to find proportions (also called relative frequencies) that describe parts of the population using ratios or decimals.

This helps us interpret the data more deeply—for example, by answering questions like, “What proportion of 6th graders prefer hot lunch?” or “What percentage of all respondents live in rural areas and prefer mountain vacations?” Understanding these values allows us to draw meaningful conclusions and identify patterns.


Key Concepts Review

  • A joint frequency is the number of individuals in a specific combination of categories.

  • A marginal frequency is the total number of individuals in one category across all groups.

  • A joint relative frequency is the joint frequency divided by the total population.

  • A marginal relative frequency is the marginal frequency divided by the total population.


Each cell inside the table represents a joint frequency. The row and column totals represent marginal frequencies, and the total number of all responses is called the total frequency.

Example 1

A survey was conducted among 280 students to find out their preferred type of school lunch and their grade level. Students were asked to choose between “Hot Lunch” and “Cold Lunch,” and to indicate whether they were in 6th or 7th grade.

  • The survey found that 95 students prefer Hot Lunch, and 120 of the students are in 7th grade.

  • The survey also showed that 50 of the 7th graders prefer Cold Lunch.

Hot Lunch Cold Lunch Total
6th Grade
7th Grade 50 120
Total 95 280 students

Question 1: What is the joint frequency and joint relative frequency for 7th-grade students who prefer Hot Lunch?

Question 2: What is the marginal frequency and marginal relative frequency for students who prefer Cold Lunch?

Solution:

  • We need to understand what joint frequency versus joint relative frequency is to be able to find them
  • Frequency is the total count from the survey, the number from the chart.
  • Relative frequency is the proportion of that count out of the total surveyed which can be expressed as a decimal or percentage.
  • So, we need to find the number of students who are 7th graders and prefer Hot lunch first.
  • We know that 50 prefer cold lunch, and there are 120 7th graders total.
    • Joint Frequency: 7th graders who prefer Hot Lunch \(=120-50=70\)
  • Now, we need to find the relative frequency by dividing that number by the total number of students surveyed, 280.
    • Relative Joint Frequency: \(=\frac{70}{280}=0.25=25%\)
  • Next, we will look at marginal frequency, which corresponds to row or column total counts.
  • In this question, we want Cold lunch marginal frequency.
  • We know that 280 total students were surveyed, and 95 prefer Hot lunches.
    • Marginal Frequency: Students who prefer Cold lunches \(=280-95=185\)
  • To find the marginal relative frequency, we will divide it by the total students surveyed.
    • Marginal Relative Frequency: \(=\frac{185}{280}=0.660714...\approx 0.661=66.1%}

Question 1 Answer: 7th graders who prefer Hot Lunch - joint frequency \(=70\) and joint relative frequency \(=25%\).

Question 2 Answer: Students who prefer Cold Lunch - marginal frequency \(=185\) and marginal relative frequency \(=66.1%\)



Example 2

A poll was conducted among 350 adults about their preferred vacation type and whether they live in an urban or rural area. Vacation types were categorized as “Beach” or “Mountains.”

  • The survey found that 198 people live in urban areas, and 143 people prefer Beach vacations.

  • The survey also showed that 90 of the rural residents prefer Mountain vacations.

Beach Mountains Total
Urban Area 198
Rural Area 90
Total 143 350 adults

Question 1: What is the joint frequency and joint relative frequency for Urban residents who prefer Beach vacations?

Question 2: What is the marginal frequency and marginal relative frequency for residents who live in rural areas?

Solution:

  • We need to understand what joint frequency versus joint relative frequency is to be able to find them
  • Frequency is the total count from the survey, the number from the chart.
  • Relative frequency is the proportion of that count out of the total surveyed, which can be expressed as a decimal or percentage.
  • So, we need to find the number of urban residents who prefer Beach vacations.
  • We will need to find how many rural residents prefer a beach vacation first. To find that, we need to know how many rural residents there are.
    • Rural residents \(=350-198=152\)
    • Rural residents who prefer Beach vacations \(=152-90=62\) 
    • Joint Frequency: Urban residents who prefer Beach vacations \(=143-62=81\)
  • Now, we need to find the relative frequency by dividing that number by the total number of residents surveyed, 350.
    • Relative Joint Frequency: \(=\frac{62}{350}=0.177142....\approx 0.177=17.7%\)
  • Next, we will look at marginal frequency, which corresponds to row or column total counts.
  • In this question, we want rural residents marginal frequency.
  • We know that 350total residents were surveyed, and 198 live in urban areas.
    • Marginal Frequency: Residents who live in Rural areas \(=350-198=152\)
  • To find the marginal relative frequency, we will divide it by the total residents surveyed.
    • Marginal Relative Frequency: \(=\frac{152}{350}=0.43428...\approx 0.434=43.4%}

Question 1 Answer: Urban residents who prefer Beach vacations - joint frequency \(=62\) and joint relative frequency \(=17.7%\).

Question 2 Answer: Residents who live in Rural areas - marginal frequency \(=152\) and marginal relative frequency \(=43.4%\)



Conclusion

Using a two-way frequency table, we can go beyond raw counts by calculating relative frequencies to understand the proportion of the population that fits within certain categories. By examining joint relative frequencies, we see how two categories overlap, while marginal relative frequencies show how one category compares to the total. These values are essential for interpreting trends and associations in survey data and help us make informed comparisons between groups.
