📚 VOCABULARY

Association: A relationship or connection between two variables where one may influence or predict the other.

Bias: A systematic error or unfair influence that affects the accuracy of data collection or interpretation.

Bivariate Data: Data that involves two different variables that can be analyzed to determine relationships.

Causal Relationship: A connection between two variables where a change in one directly causes a change in the other.

Categorical Data: Data that can be sorted into groups or categories, such as types, labels, or classifications.

Causation: A relationship in which one variable directly causes a change in another variable.

Confounding Variable: An outside influence that affects both variables being studied, possibly giving a false impression of a relationship.

Correlation: A statistical relationship or pattern between two variables, showing that they tend to vary together.

Lurking Variable: A hidden variable that is not part of the study but affects both observed variables.

Numerical Data: Data that consists of numbers and can be measured or counted.

Spurious Correlation: A false correlation where two variables appear related but are actually linked by coincidence or a third factor.

Univariate Data: Data that involves only one variable and describes a single characteristic or measurement.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore the important distinction between correlation and causation. This topic is essential for understanding how to interpret data correctly and avoid making false assumptions. We often see data presented in graphs or studies that show two variables moving together—this is known as correlation. However, just because two things occur at the same time or seem to be related does not mean that one caused the other. This lesson will help you understand when there is simply a correlation and when causation is more likely.


Understanding Correlation

Correlation is a pattern or relationship between two variables. Correlations can be positive (both variables increase together), negative (one increases while the other decreases), or show no pattern at all. But remember: correlation only tells us about a pattern — not about a cause.

  • Example: if students who study more tend to get better grades, we might say there is a positive correlation between study time and test scores.


Difference Between Correlation and Causation

Causation means that one variable causes the other to change. This is a much stronger relationship than correlation.

  • It requires evidence, often through scientific experiments or logical analysis, that changes in one variable directly result in changes in the other.
  • Many times, two variables may seem connected, but there is actually a lurking or confounding variable that influences both.
  • In these cases, assuming causation would be misleading.


Why Causation Cannot Be Assumed

Even when two variables show a strong correlation, we cannot immediately say one caused the other.

  • For example, there might be a correlation between eating ice cream and the number of sunburns, but that doesn’t mean ice cream causes sunburn.
  • A third factor—like hot summer weather—could be the reason both occur at the same time.

Recognizing this helps us become smarter consumers of data and avoid drawing false conclusions.


Analyzing Real-World Scenarios

To determine if a correlation exists in a real-world scenario, we need to examine if changes in one variable are associated with consistent changes in another. Then, we evaluate whether there’s enough evidence to believe that one causes the other—or if a third variable is involved. Relationships can usually be sorted into three categories:

  • Possible causation: There is reasonable evidence that one variable may cause the other, but there is a potential for a third variable.

  • Unlikely causation: A third variable is very likely involved, making the correlation coincidental.

  • Likely causation: There is strong support or experimental evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship.



Example 1

A study finds that students who sleep more tend to score higher on tests.

What best describes the relationship between these variables?

A. No correlation; no causation
B. Positive correlation; causation unlikely
C. Positive correlation; possible causation
D. Negative correlation; likely causation

Solution:

  • This example shows a positive correlation because as sleep increases, test scores also increase. That means both variables move in the same direction.
  • Now, regarding causation, it is possible that getting more sleep causes improved test performance. After all, sleep supports brain function, memory, and concentration—all of which are important for doing well on tests.
  • However, we can’t say for certain that more sleep directly causes better scores because other factors (like student motivation, study habits, or access to resources) could also play a role.
  • Still, there is a plausible biological connection that makes causation possible.

Answer: Option C



Example 2

A graph shows that as the number of hours playing video games increases, grades decrease.

What best explains this pattern?

A. Negative correlation; causation is likely
B. Positive correlation; causation is unlikely
C. Negative correlation; causation is unlikely
D. No correlation; no causation

Solution:

  • This scenario shows a negative correlation because as video game time increases, grades decrease—the variables move in opposite directions.
  • While this suggests a pattern, it does not necessarily mean that video games cause bad grades.
  • It could be that students who already struggle academically are using video games as an escape, or that poor time management, lack of parental supervision, or lower motivation are influencing both behaviors.
  • These are examples of lurking or confounding variables that might explain the trend. Therefore, although a relationship exists, causation is unlikely without more controlled evidence.

Answer: Option C



Conclusion

Understanding the difference between correlation and causation is critical when interpreting data. Just because two variables move together doesn’t mean one causes the other. By carefully analyzing patterns and thinking critically about third variables, you can avoid drawing false conclusions and become a more accurate and thoughtful data interpreter. This lesson gives you the tools to evaluate real-world relationships and classify them based on the strength and likelihood of causation.
