📚 VOCABULARY

Association: A pattern or relationship observed between two variables in a data set.

Bivariate Categorical Data: Data that involves two different categorical variables measured on the same individuals.

Column Marginal: The total of a column in a two-way table representing the total relative or actual frequency for a category in the column variable.

Conditional Relative Frequency: The proportion of individuals in a subcategory given a condition on one variable, found by dividing a joint frequency by its row or column total.

Frequency: The count of how often a value or category appears in a data set.

Joint Frequency: The number of individuals that fall into both of two categorical variables' categories.

Marginal Frequency: The total count for a single category across one variable, regardless of the other variable.

Marginal Relative Frequency: A marginal frequency expressed as a proportion of the total.

Percentages: A representation of a portion out of 100.

Population: The entire group being studied in a survey or data collection effort.

Probability: A number between 0 and 1 that expresses the likelihood of a particular event occurring.

Relative Frequency: A frequency written as a fraction, decimal, or percentage of the total number of observations.

Row Marginal: The total of a row in a two-way table representing the total relative or actual frequency for a category in the row variable.

Total Frequency: The grand total of all frequencies in the table; usually represents the entire population or sample.

Two-Way Frequency Table: A table that organizes data for two categorical variables, displaying how often combinations of categories occur.

Two-Way Relative Frequency Table: A two-way table in which all values are expressed as relative frequencies (fractions, decimals, or percentages), showing proportions of the total.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to construct a two-way relative frequency table using given marginal and conditional relative frequencies. These tables allow us to visualize how two categorical variables relate using proportions instead of raw counts. While a two-way frequency table shows actual numbers, a two-way relative frequency table represents percentages or proportions of the total population, which makes comparisons easier and more meaningful.

We’ll review what relative frequencies are and then learn how to build a complete table using partial relative data. This skill is especially useful when working with studies, surveys, or medical or social science reports that summarize findings in terms of probabilities or percentages.



How to Construct a Two-Way Relative Frequency Table

Step 1: Identify the row or column marginals

Start by labeling the table with the row and column categories and entering the marginal relative frequencies (usually totals for each row or column). These are typically given as percentages and always sum to 100%.

Step 2: Use conditional relative frequencies to find joint cells

Multiply the conditional relative frequencies by the marginal total of the group to find each joint relative frequency.

Step 3: Subtract to find complementary values

Once you have one joint frequency in a row or column, subtract from the marginal to find the other.

Step 4: Repeat for the second category

Apply the same process using the other condition.

Step 5: Fill in the column totals

Add down columns to compute marginal column totals. They should sum to 100% when added with the row marginals.



Example 1

A report summarizes the study habits of students as follows:

  • 60% of students study at least 5 hours per week, and 40% do not.

  • Of the students who study at least 5 hours, 85% complete all their homework.

  • Of the students who study less than 5 hours, 50% complete all their homework.

Construct a two-way relative frequency table.

Solution:

We begin by filling in the information from the first bullet into our table.

Completes Homework Does Not Complete Homework Total
Studies \(\geq 5\) hours 60%
Students \(<5\) hours 40%
Total 100%

Next, we can use the information in the second bullet.

  • Of the students who study at least 5 hours (60%), 85% complete all their homework.
  • This tells us that 85% of the 60% which mathematically means \(0.85\cdot 0.60=0.51\cdot 100=51%\)
  • We also know since the row is 60% total and the first cell in the top row is now 51%, we can find the second cell in the top row by subtracting.
  • \(60%-51%=9%\)

We can add this information to our table now to complete the values in the row for studies at least 5 hours.

Completes Homework Does Not Complete Homework Total
Studies \(\geq 5\) hours 51% 9% 60%
Students \(<5\) hours 40%
Total 100%

Last, we will use the third bullet to now find the relative frequencies for the 2nd row, students who study less than 5 hours.

  • Of the students who study less than 5 hours (40%), 50% complete all their homework.
  • This tells us that 50% of 40% goes in the first cell for that row.
  • \(0.50\cdot 0.40=0.20\cdot 100=20%\)
  • Then we subtract that from 40% to find the relative frequency for the 2nd cell.
  • \(40%-20%=20%\)

Now we can complete our table and find the column total percentages.

Completes Homework Does Not Complete Homework Total
Studies \(\geq 5\) hours 51% 9% 60%
Students \(<5\) hours 20% 20% 40%
Total 71% 29% 100%



Example 2

A political science survey summarizes:

  • 70% of registered voters saw at least one political ad; the remaining 30% did not.

  • Among those who saw at least one ad, 40% voted in the election.

  • Among those who did not see an ad, 25% voted.

Construct a two-way relative frequency table.

Solution:

We begin by filling in the information from the first bullet into our table.

Voted Didn't Vote Total
Saw Political Ad 70%
Didn't See Political Ad 30%
Total 100%

Next, we can use the information in the second bullet.

  • Among those who saw at least one add (70%), 40% voted in the election.
  • This tells us that 40% of the 70% which mathematically means \(0.40\cdot 0.70=0.28\cdot 100=28%\)
  • We also know since the row is 70% total and the first cell in that row is now 28%, we can find the second cell in the top row by subtracting.
  • \(70%-28%=42%\)

We can add this information to our table now to complete the values in the row for saw political ads.

Voted Didn't Vote Total
Saw Political Ad 28% 42% 70%
Didn't See Political Ad 30%
Total 100%

Last, we will use the third bullet to now find the relative frequencies for the didn't see ads row.

  • Among those who did not see an ad (30%), 25% voted.
  • This tells us that 25% of 30% goes in the first cell for that row.
  • \(0.25\cdot 0.30=0.075\cdot 100=7.5%\)
  • Then we subtract that from 30% to find the relative frequency for the 2nd cell.
  • \(30%-7.5%=22.5%\)

Now we can complete our table and find the column total percentages.

Voted Didn't Vote Total
Saw Political Ad 28% 42% 70%
Didn't See Political Ad 7.5% 22.5% 30%
Total 35.5% 64.5% 100%



Conclusion

Two-way relative frequency tables give us a way to express relationships between two categorical variables using proportions or percentages, which is helpful when comparing groups of different sizes. By using marginal and conditional relative frequencies, we can calculate how categories overlap and clearly see trends in the data. Understanding how to construct and interpret these tables is a powerful tool for analyzing and communicating real-world survey results.
