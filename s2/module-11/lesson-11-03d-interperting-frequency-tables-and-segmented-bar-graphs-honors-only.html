📚 VOCABULARY

Association: A pattern or relationship observed between two variables in a data set.

Bivariate Categorical Data: Data that involves two different categorical variables measured on the same individuals.

Column Marginal: The total of a column in a two-way table representing the total relative or actual frequency for a category in the column variable.

Conditional Relative Frequency: The proportion of individuals in a subcategory given a condition on one variable, found by dividing a joint frequency by its row or column total.

Frequency: The count of how often a value or category appears in a data set.

Joint Frequency: The number of individuals that fall into both of two categorical variables' categories.

Marginal Frequency: The total count for a single category across one variable, regardless of the other variable.

Marginal Relative Frequency: A marginal frequency expressed as a proportion of the total.

Percentages: A representation of a portion out of 100.

Population: The entire group being studied in a survey or data collection effort.

Probability: A number between 0 and 1 that expresses the likelihood of a particular event occurring.

Relative Frequency: A frequency written as a fraction, decimal, or percentage of the total number of observations.

Row Marginal: The total of a row in a two-way table representing the total relative or actual frequency for a category in the row variable.

Total Frequency: The grand total of all frequencies in the table; usually represents the entire population or sample.

Two-Way Frequency Table: A table that organizes data for two categorical variables, displaying how often combinations of categories occur.

Two-Way Relative Frequency Table: A two-way table in which all values are expressed as relative frequencies (fractions, decimals, or percentages), showing proportions of the total.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to read and interpret two-way relative frequency tables and segmented bar graphs — two powerful tools for analyzing relationships between two categorical variables. You will learn to distinguish between three types of relative frequencies — joint, marginal, and conditional — and to explain what each one tells us in the context of a real-world situation. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to draw meaningful conclusions about data and recognize when an association between two categories may exist.

What is a Two-Way Relative Frequency Table

A two-way relative frequency table organizes data about two categorical variables at the same time. Instead of showing raw counts, it shows proportions — each cell value represents a fraction of the total number of observations. All the values in the table add up to 1.00 (or 100%).

Every value you see in the table falls into one of three categories: a joint relative frequency, a marginal relative frequency, or a conditional relative frequency. Learning to tell these apart — and knowing what each one means in context — is the central skill of this lesson.


The Three Types of Relative Frequencies

Joint Relative Frequency A joint relative frequency is found in the interior cells of the table — not in the Total row or Total column. It tells you the proportion of the entire group that belongs to both categories at the same time. When you interpret it, use the word "and." Marginal Relative

Frequency A marginal relative frequency is found in the Total row or Total column — along the margins of the table. It tells you the proportion of the entire group that belongs to one category, regardless of the other variable. These are sometimes called the "row totals" and "column totals."

Conditional Relative Frequency A conditional relative frequency tells you the proportion within a specific subgroup — that is, given that a condition is already met, what fraction also satisfies something else. You compute it by dividing an interior cell by either its row total or its column total. When you interpret it, use the phrase "given that" or "of those who."


Interpreting Relative Frequencies Given a Two-Way Table

A survey of 500 high school students asked whether they participate in a school club and whether they feel connected to their school community. The results are shown in the two-way relative frequency table below.

Feels Connected Does Not Feel Connected Total
In a Club 0.42 0.18 0.60
Not in a Club 0.13 0.27 0.40
Total 0.55 0.45 1.00

Identifying and interpreting joint relative frequencies: 

The four interior cells are joint relative frequencies.

  • For example, the value 0.42 is in the "In a Club" row and "Feels Connected" column.
    • This means that 42% of all students surveyed are both in a club and feel connected to their school community.
  • Similarly, 0.27 is in the "Not in a Club" row and "Does Not Feel Connected" column.
    • This means that 27% of all students surveyed are both not in a club and do not feel connected.


Identifying and interpreting marginal relative frequencies: 

The Total row and Total column contain the marginal relative frequencies.

  • The value 0.60 tells us that 60% of all students surveyed are in a club, regardless of how they feel about school connection.
  • The value 0.55 in the Total row tells us that 55% of all students surveyed feel connected to their school, regardless of club participation.


Computing and interpreting a conditional relative frequency: 

Suppose we want to know: of students who are in a club, what proportion feel connected?

  • We divide the joint frequency by the row total: \( \dfrac{0.42}{0.60} = 0.70 \).
  • This means 70% of students who are in a club feel connected to their school community.
  • Compare this to students not in a club: \( \dfrac{0.13}{0.40} = 0.325 \), meaning only about 32.5% of students not in a club feel connected.
  • The large difference in these conditional frequencies suggests that club participation and feeling connected are associated.

 

How to Spot an Association

When conditional relative frequencies differ noticeably between groups, it suggests an association between the two variables.

If the conditional frequencies were nearly equal, the two variables would appear unrelated (independent).

Example 1

A survey asked 400 middle school students whether they prefer reading physical books or e-books, and whether they read for at least 20 minutes per day. The two-way relative frequency table is shown below.

Reads 20+ min/day Reads less than 20 min/day Total
Prefers Physical Books 0.36 0.24 0.60
Prefers E-books 0.14 0.26 0.40
Total 0.50 0.50 1.00

Answer each question below, then expand the solution to check your work.

(a) Identify the joint relative frequency for students who prefer physical books and read 20 or more minutes per day. Interpret it in context.

(b) Identify the marginal relative frequency for students who prefer e-books. Interpret it in context.

(c) Compute the conditional relative frequency: of students who prefer physical books, what proportion reads 20 or more minutes per day? Then compute the same conditional frequency for students who prefer e-books. Do the results suggest an association? Explain.

Show Solution

Solution (a): The joint relative frequency is 0.36. This value is in the interior of the table, at the intersection of "Prefers Physical Books" and "Reads 20+ min/day." It means that 36% of all students surveyed both prefer physical books and read at least 20 minutes per day.

Solution (b): The marginal relative frequency for students who prefer e-books is 0.40, found in the Total column of the "Prefers E-books" row. This means that 40% of all students surveyed prefer e-books, regardless of how long they read each day.

Solution (c): For physical book readers: \( \dfrac{0.36}{0.60} = 0.60 \). So 60% of students who prefer physical books read 20 or more minutes per day.

For e-book readers: \( \dfrac{0.14}{0.40} = 0.35 \). So 35% of students who prefer e-books read 20 or more minutes per day.

Because these conditional frequencies differ substantially (60% vs. 35%), the data suggest an association between book format preference and daily reading time. Students who prefer physical books appear more likely to read 20 or more minutes per day than those who prefer e-books.



Example 2

A health survey of adults asked whether they exercise regularly (at least 3 times per week) and whether they reported getting adequate sleep (7 or more hours per night). The results are summarized in the table below.

Adequate Sleep Inadequate Sleep Total
Exercises Regularly 0.45 0.15 0.60
Does Not Exercise Regularly 0.20 0.20 0.40
Total 0.65 0.35 1.00

(a) What does the value 0.20 in the "Does Not Exercise Regularly / Inadequate Sleep" cell represent? Interpret it in context.

(b) What does the marginal relative frequency of 0.65 represent in context?

(c) Compute the conditional relative frequency for adequate sleep given that a person exercises regularly. Then compute it for those who do not exercise regularly. Based on these values, does there appear to be an association between exercise habits and sleep quality? Explain.

Show Solution

Solution (a): The value 0.20 is a joint relative frequency. It means that 20% of all adults surveyed both do not exercise regularly and get inadequate sleep.

Solution (b): The value 0.65 is a marginal relative frequency in the Total row. It means that 65% of all adults surveyed reported getting adequate sleep, regardless of whether they exercise regularly.

Solution (c): Among those who exercise regularly: \( \dfrac{0.45}{0.60} = 0.75 \). So 75% of adults who exercise regularly get adequate sleep.

Among those who do not exercise regularly: \( \dfrac{0.20}{0.40} = 0.50 \). So 50% of adults who do not exercise regularly get adequate sleep.

The conditional frequencies are noticeably different (75% vs. 50%), which suggests an association between regular exercise and adequate sleep. Adults who exercise regularly appear more likely to get adequate sleep than those who do not.



What is a Segmented Bar Graph?

A segmented bar graph (also called a stacked bar chart) is a visual display of the same information contained in a two-way relative frequency table. Each bar represents one category of the horizontal variable, and the bar is divided into colored segments — one for each category of the second variable. The height of each segment corresponds to a relative frequency, and each complete bar reaches a total height of 1.00 (or 100%).

Segmented bar graphs make it especially easy to compare conditional relative frequencies across groups at a glance. When the segments within each bar are roughly the same size across all bars, the two variables are likely not associated. When the segment sizes differ noticeably from bar to bar, an association may be present.


Reading a Segmented Bar Graph

Where to Find Each Type of Frequency Joint relative frequency: The height (proportion) of one colored segment within one bar — that is, the proportion of the entire group that falls in both categories.

Marginal relative frequency: The total height of one full bar (always 1.00 in a relative frequency segmented bar), or the overall proportion of one category read from a separate summary. The horizontal axis category labels tell you which group each bar represents.

Conditional relative frequency: The height of one segment within a specific bar — read directly off the vertical axis. It tells you the proportion within that bar's group that belongs to the given segment's category.



Interpreting a Segmented Bar Graph

The segmented bar graph below displays data from a survey of high school students about their preferred mode of transportation to school and whether they are usually on time. Each bar represents a transportation type, and each segment shows the proportion of that group who arrive on time (blue) vs. arrive late (purple).

Interpreting conditional relative frequencies from the graph: Each bar represents a transportation group, and the height of each segment is a conditional relative frequency. For example, the blue segment of the Bus bar reaches 0.65, meaning 65% of students who ride the bus arrive on time. The Car bar's blue segment reaches 0.80, meaning 80% of students who ride in a car arrive on time. For Bike/Walk, it is 0.55.

Identifying an association: The conditional frequencies vary across transportation modes — from 55% to 80% on time. This variation suggests an association between transportation mode and punctuality. Students who ride in a car appear most likely to arrive on time, while those who bike or walk appear least likely.

What about joint and marginal frequencies? A segmented bar graph in this standard form (where each bar reaches 1.00) displays conditional relative frequencies within each bar directly. To find marginal or joint relative frequencies, you would also need to know the proportion each transportation group makes up of the whole. If the graph were accompanied by a note that, say, 40% take the bus, 35% ride in a car, and 25% bike or walk, you could multiply: the joint frequency for "Bus and On Time" would be \( 0.40 \times 0.65 = 0.26 \).

Example 3

The segmented bar graph below shows survey results from a sample of students about their study location preference (library vs. home) and whether they completed all of their assignments in the past week.

(a) What conditional relative frequency does the dark green segment of the Library bar represent? Interpret it in context.

(b) What conditional relative frequency does the light green segment of the Home bar represent? Interpret it in context.

(c) A classmate says, "This graph shows that more students who study in the library completed all their assignments than students who study at home." Is this statement correctly interpreting the graph? Explain why or why not, and write a corrected or clarified interpretation.

(d) Based on the graph, does there appear to be an association between study location and assignment completion? Explain using the conditional relative frequencies.

Show Solution

Solution (a): The dark green segment of the Library bar reaches a height of 0.78. This is a conditional relative frequency meaning 78% of students who study in the library completed all of their assignments in the past week.

Solution (b): The light green segment of the Home bar reaches a height of 0.50. This is a conditional relative frequency meaning 50% of students who study at home did not complete all of their assignments in the past week.

Solution (c): The statement is not quite correct as written. The graph shows proportions within each group, not counts. The correct interpretation is: a greater proportion of students who study in the library completed all assignments (78%) compared to students who study at home (50%). Without knowing how many students are in each group, we cannot conclude that more students (in terms of raw count) in the library group completed their work.

Solution (d): Yes, the data suggest an association between study location and assignment completion. The conditional frequency for completing all assignments is 78% for library studiers compared to 50% for home studiers — a difference of 28 percentage points. Because the segment heights differ substantially between the two bars, the two variables appear to be related. Students who study in the library are proportionally more likely to complete all of their assignments.



Example 4

The segmented bar graph below shows data from a survey of adults about whether they drink coffee daily and whether they describe themselves as a "morning person." Each bar represents one group, and segments indicate the proportion who self-identify as a morning person (dark) vs. not (light).

(a) What does the dark purple segment of the "Daily Coffee Drinker" bar represent? Interpret it in context.

(b) The survey also reported that 60% of adults in the sample are daily coffee drinkers and 40% are not. Use this information and the graph to compute the joint relative frequency for adults who are daily coffee drinkers and identify as morning people. Show your work.

(c) Compute the joint relative frequency for adults who do not drink coffee daily and do not identify as morning people. Show your work.

(d) Does the graph suggest an association between daily coffee consumption and identifying as a morning person? Use conditional relative frequencies from the graph to support your answer.

Show Solution

Solution (a): The dark purple segment of the Daily Coffee Drinker bar has a height of 0.72. This is a conditional relative frequency: 72% of adults who drink coffee daily identify as morning people.

Solution (b): The marginal relative frequency for daily coffee drinkers is 0.60. The conditional frequency for being a morning person given daily coffee drinking is 0.72. Multiplying: \( 0.60 \times 0.72 = 0.432 \). So the joint relative frequency is 0.432, meaning approximately 43.2% of all adults surveyed both drink coffee daily and identify as morning people.

Solution (c): The marginal relative frequency for non-daily coffee drinkers is 0.40. The conditional frequency for not being a morning person given no daily coffee habit is 0.62. Multiplying: \( 0.40 \times 0.62 = 0.248 \). So the joint relative frequency is 0.248, meaning approximately 24.8% of all adults surveyed neither drink coffee daily nor identify as morning people.

Solution (d): Yes, the graph suggests a strong association between daily coffee consumption and morning preference. Among daily coffee drinkers, 72% identify as morning people, compared to only 38% of non-daily coffee drinkers — a difference of 34 percentage points. Because the segment heights are very different between the two bars, the two variables appear to be associated. Adults who drink coffee daily are substantially more likely to identify as morning people than those who do not.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you developed the skills to read and interpret two-way relative frequency tables and segmented bar graphs in context. You learned that joint relative frequencies describe the proportion of an entire group that shares two characteristics at once, marginal relative frequencies describe the overall proportion belonging to a single category, and conditional relative frequencies describe proportions within a specific subgroup — found in the interior cells of a table or in the height of a segment within a single bar on a graph. Most importantly, you learned to use conditional relative frequencies as a tool for identifying associations: when the conditional frequencies for one outcome differ noticeably across groups, it is evidence that the two categorical variables may be related. In the next lesson, you will apply these interpretation skills to construct two-way relative frequency tables from raw data and from conditional information, building the full picture of bivariate categorical analysis.
