📚 VOCABULARY

Association: A relationship between two variables that suggests a pattern or connection.

Bivariate Categorical Data: Data that involves two categorical variables, each with two or more categories.

Column Marginal: The total frequency for each column in a two-way table, found at the bottom of each column.

Frequency: The number of times a category or pair of categories appears in a data set.

Joint Frequencies: The values inside the table that represent the frequency for a specific combination of two categories.

Marginal Frequencies: The totals of each row or column that represent the sum of joint frequencies along the edges of the table.

Percentages: A value expressed out of 100 that shows how large one quantity is compared to another.

Population: The complete group of individuals or items being studied in a survey or experiment.

Probability: A number between 0 and 1 that expresses the likelihood of an event occurring.

Relative Frequency: The ratio of a frequency to the total number of observations.

Row Marginal: The total frequency for each row in a two-way table, found at the far right of each row.

Total Frequency: The grand total of all data entries, typically found in the bottom-right cell of a two-way table.

Two-Way Frequency Table: A table that displays frequencies for two categorical variables, allowing you to examine relationships between them.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today’s lesson, we will explore how to organize and interpret bivariate categorical data using a two-way frequency table. These tables allow us to compare two different categories—like favorite social media platforms and gender—by organizing frequencies into rows and columns. When surveys or polls collect data on two different questions or characteristics, a two-way table helps us summarize the results and spot patterns or associations.

We will learn how to construct a complete two-way frequency table from a few given values such as the total population, row and column totals, and specific cell frequencies. Understanding how to use the structure of the table to fill in missing values is a key skill in data analysis and lays the groundwork for calculating relative frequencies later in more advanced settings.


What is a Two-Way Frequency Table?

A two-way frequency table is a table that displays the frequencies of combinations of two categorical variables.

  • For example, in a survey where people are asked both their gender and their favorite social media platform, each response falls into one of the combined categories (e.g., Male–Instagram, Female–TikTok). The table helps organize these responses clearly.

Each cell inside the table represents a joint frequency. The row and column totals represent marginal frequencies, and the total number of all responses is called the total frequency.

Example 1

A survey was conducted among 280 students to find out their preferred type of school lunch and their grade level. Students were asked to choose between “Hot Lunch” and “Cold Lunch,” and to indicate whether they were in 6th or 7th grade.

  • The survey found that 95 students prefer Hot Lunch, and 120 of the students are in 7th grade.

  • The survey also showed that 50 of the 7th graders prefer Cold Lunch.

Task:
Use the information to complete the two-way frequency table.

Solution:

  • We can begin by filling in the Total Population Surveyed in the very last bottom cell of the chart. We will also fill in the categories and populations in the header columns and rows.
  • Hot Lunch Cold Lunch Total
    6th Grade
    8th Grade
    Total 280 students
  • Now we can use the information given in the bullets of some specific totals to fill in a few cells.
  • Hot Lunch Cold Lunch Total
    6th Grade
    7th Grade 50 120
    Total 95 280 students
  • Now we will use the numbers we do know and the totals we know to fill in the other cells.
  • We can first fill in the total for 6th graders. We know there are 120 total 7th graders and 280 total students.
    • Total 6th graders \(=280-120=160\)
  • Next, we can find how many total students prefer cold lunches.
    • Total cold lunches \(=280-95=185\)
  • We can fill in those totals to give us a better picture of the table and what is left to find.
  • Hot Lunch Cold Lunch Total
    6th Grade 160
    7th Grade 50 120
    Total 95 185 280
  • Now, we can use the totals to find the remaining cells.
  • We know 50 7th graders prefer a cold lunch and there are 120 total 7th graders.
    • 7th graders who prefer hot lunch \(=120-50=70\)
  • We also know that 185 students prefer cold lunches and 50 are 7th graders.
    • 6th graders who prefer cold lunch \(=185-50=135\)
  • Last, we can find how many 6th graders prefer hot lunch since there are 160 total 6th graders.
    • 6th graders who prefer hot lunch \(=160-135=25\)

The values in green are the joint frequencies and the values in blue are the marginal frequencies. Understanding these values will help us in the next lesson with finding joint and marginal relative frequencies.



Example 2

A poll was conducted among 350 adults about their preferred vacation type and whether they live in an urban or rural area. Vacation types were categorized as “Beach” or “Mountains.”

  • The survey found that 198 people live in urban areas, and 143 people prefer Beach vacations.

  • The survey also showed that 90 of the rural residents prefer Mountain vacations.

Task:
Use the information to complete the two-way frequency table.

Solution:

  • We can begin by filling in the Total Population Surveyed in the very last bottom cell of the chart. We will also fill in the categories and populations in the header columns and rows.
  • Beach Mountains Total
    Urban Area

    Total 350 adults
  • Now we can use the information given in the bullets of some specific totals to fill in a few cells.
  • Beach Mountains Total
    Urban Area 198

  • Now we will use the numbers we do know and the totals we know to fill in the other cells.
  • We can first fill in the total for rural area adults. We know there are 198 urban area adults and 350 total adults.
    • Total rural area adults \(=350-198=152\)
  • Next, we can find how many total adults prefer vacations in mountains.
    • Total who prefer mountains \(=350-143=207\)
  • We can fill in those totals to give us a better picture of the table and what is left to find.
  • Beach Mountains Total
    Urban Area 198
    Rural Area 90 152
    Total 143 207 350 adults

  • Last, we can find how many adults live in urban areas and prefer beach vacations.
    • Adults in urban areas who prefer beach vacations \(=198-117=81\)

Conclusion

Constructing a two-way frequency table is an essential tool in organizing categorical data. When we break down two variables—such as preferences and groups—into categories and count how often they occur together, we can see trends and patterns more easily. Using information like joint frequencies, marginal totals, and the overall population size allows us to logically fill in the table and interpret survey results accurately. Mastering this skill sets the foundation for deeper statistical understanding in future lessons.
