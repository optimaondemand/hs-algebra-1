Decay factor: The constant proportion by which a quantity decreases during each unit of time or other equal intervals.

Equation: A mathematical statement that equates two expressions or quantities.

Graph: A visual representation of data or a mathematical function, typically shown on a coordinate plane with x- and y-axes.

Half-Life: The time it takes for half of a quantity, such as a radioactive isotope or a drug concentration, to decay or decrease by half of its initial value.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In the last lesson, we learned about exponential growth functions and their features. In this lesson, we will explore exponential decay functions and their features.

In this lesson, we'll explore exponential decay functions, contrasting them with exponential growth, and learn how to identify exponential decay from both graphs and equations. Exponential decay describes a process where quantities decrease over time at a rate proportional to their current value.


Before diving into exponential decay functions, let's briefly recap the key features of exponential growth functions. Exponential growth occurs when a quantity increases at a constant percentage rate, usually over time. It is often represented by the equation \(y=ab^x\), where 'a' is the initial value, 'b' is the base (also called "the growth factor"), and 'x' is the input variable (since this variable is often time you are likely to see this function in the form \(y=ab^x\)).

Now, let's explore the fascinating world of exponential decay functions, where quantities decrease over time at a constant percentage rate. What is an Exponential Decay Function?

An exponential decay function models a decrease where the rate of change reduces exponentially. The general form of an exponential function is \(y=a(b)^x\), where:

  • \(y\) is the final amount,
  • \(a\) is the initial amount when \(x=0\),
  • \(b\) is the base of the exponential function, representing the decay factor per unit decrease in \(x\),
  • \(x\) represents time or another independent variable.

For a function to represent exponential decay, \(0<b<1\). This ensures that as x increases, y decreases at a decelerating rate.

One more important thing about the base in exponential decay functions: while the b value that is strictly between 0 and 1 is called "the decay factor," you will often come across references to "the decay rate." The decay factor and decay rate are NOT the same thing. The decay factor is the base that is multiplied by itself x (or t) number of times. But the decay rate is difference value of 1 – b (that is, 1 minus the decay factor).

For example, an exponential function with a decay factor (b) of \(75%\) (\(0.75\)) has a decay rate of \(25%\) (\(1-0.75=0.25\)). The initial value decays at a rate of \(25%\) per input x. Again, compare this to exponential growth functions.

Finally, x is the input variable. Since when dealing with decay functions the input variable is often time elapsed, you will also commonly see the decay function written as \(y=ab^t\).


Let's look at a few examples.

Example

Does the function \(y=10(0.8)^t\), where 't' represents time in years, represent exponential decay?

Here, the initial value (a) is 10, and the decay rate is 0.2 (or 20%). Since this function has a decay rate of 20% it has a decay factor of 80% (0.8). We can evaluate this function for various values of t to observe the decay process.

  • For \(t=0\), \(y=10(0.8)^0=10\) (initial value, no decay).
  • For \(t=1\), \(y=10(0.8)^1=8\) (20% decay).
  • For \(t=2\), \(y=10(0.8)^2=6.4\) (36% decay).


Another example is the function \(y=500(0.5)^t\), where 't' represents time in hours. Here, the initial value (a) is 500, and the decay rate is 0.5 (or 50%). We can calculate the values of 'y' for different 't' to understand the decay process.

  • For \(t=0\), \(y=500(0.5)^0=500\) (initial value, no decay).
  • For \(t=1\), \(y=500(0.5)^1=250\) (50% decay).
  • For \(t=2\), \(y=500(0.5)^2=125\) (75% decay).


Determining Exponential Decay from Equations

To confirm if an equation represents exponential decay, verify the following:

  1. Equation Form: The equation should be in the form \(y=a(b)^x\).
  2. Base b: The base \(b\) must be between 0 and 1 (\(0<b<1\)). This is critical for modeling decay.
  3. Initial Amount a: The initial amount a should be positive, indicating the starting point is above zero.


Example 1: Exponential Decay Function

The given function represents an exponential function. Does the function represent exponential decay?

Given Function: \(y=100(0.5)^x\)

Solution:

Analysis:

  • \(a=100\), the initial amount.
  • \(b=0.5\), which is between 0 and 1.

This function represents exponential decay because the base \(b=0.5\) causes the value of y to halve as x increases by 1. The decay rate is constant, indicating that the quantity decreases over time at a rate proportional to its current value.


Identifying Exponential Decay on Graphs

An exponential decay function on a graph will have the following features:

  1. Downward Slope: The graph slopes downwards from left to right, indicating a decrease in y as x increases.
  2. Decreasing Rate of Change: The graph shows a decelerating rate of decrease; the curve flattens as you move from left to right.
  3. Y-Intercept: The graph crosses the y-axis at \(y=a\), where a is the initial amount, and it should be positive (\(a>0\)).
  4. Asymptote: There is a horizontal asymptote, typically along the x-axis (\(y=0\)), which the graph approaches but never crosses as x increases.


Example 2

The graph shown represents an exponential function. Does the function represent exponential decay?

Solution:

Explanation:

  • The graph shows a downward slope from left to right.
  • As x approaches infinity, y approaches 0 but never reaches it, indicating a horizontal asymptote at \(y=0\).

This graph represents exponential decay. The end behavior, where y decreases as x increases and approaches zero, confirms that the function models a process of decreasing over time at a rate proportional to the current value.


Conclusion

Understanding the difference between exponential growth and decay is crucial in Algebra 1. Exponential decay functions model processes where quantities decrease over time at rates proportional to their current values. By examining the base of the exponential function in an equation and the slope and end behavior of its graph, students can effectively identify and interpret exponential decay in various contexts. This knowledge is invaluable for analyzing real-world phenomena and mathematical problems alike.
