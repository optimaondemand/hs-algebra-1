📚 VOCABULARY

Annual Percentage Rate (APR): The annualized interest rate charged on a loan or investment, expressed as a percentage. It represents the cost of borrowing or the potential return on investment, excluding compounding effects.

Annual percentage yield (APY): The effective interest rate that takes into account the compounding periods per year, providing a more accurate measure of the true return on an investment.

Compound interest: The type of interest that is calculated on the initial principal as well as the accumulated interest from previous periods, resulting in exponential growth.

Compound interest formula: The mathematical equation used to calculate the future value of an investment or loan that takes into account the principal, interest rate, compounding periods, and time period.

Compounding periods: The number of times interest is compounded per unit of time, such as annually, semi-annually, quarterly, or monthly. See also 'Frequency'.

Exponential growth: A type of growth in which the quantity or value increases rapidly over time, following an exponential function.

Final amount: The total value of an investment or loan, including the principal and accumulated compound interest, at the end of a specified time period.

Financial context: The specific financial situation or scenario in which concepts, calculations, or decisions related to money and finance are applied.

Fixed deposit account: A type of investment account offered by banks or financial institutions where a fixed amount of money is deposited for a specific time period at a predetermined interest rate.

Frequency : The number of compounding periods within a given time frame. It determines how often the interest is calculated and added to the principal. Common frequencies include annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, or daily. See also 'Compounding periods'.

Future value: The total amount of money, including principal and accumulated interest, that an investment will grow to after a specified time period.

Interest rate: The percentage or decimal value that represents the cost of borrowing money or the return on an investment, per unit of time.

Principal: The initial amount of money on which simple interest is calculated or the original amount borrowed or invested.

Simple interest: The amount of money earned or paid on a loan or investment that is calculated based on the initial amount (principal), the interest rate, and the time period.

Time period: The length of time for which interest is calculated or the duration of a loan or investment, usually measured in years.

Compound interest is a powerful financial principle that allows your money to grow faster over time. It's not just about earning interest on your initial investment (the principal) but also earning interest on the interest that has been added to your investment.

Let's explore compound interest, which is a more powerful concept that considers both the initial investment and the accumulated interest. In this lesson, we will define compound interest and thoroughly explore its formula. We'll also include worked-out examples, both mathematical and real-world, to help solidify your understanding.


Compound Interest: An Overview

Compound interest is calculated on the initial principal and also on the accumulated interest of previous periods. This means that your investment grows exponentially, as each period's interest is added to the principal, forming a new base for the next period's interest calculation.

Connection to Exponential Functions

The reason compound interest is related to exponential functions is due to its nature of growth. An exponential function is defined as \(f(x)=ab^x\), where \(a\) is the initial value, \(b\) is the base representing the growth factor, and  represents time or the number of periods. Similarly, the compound interest formula is an example of exponential growth, with the amount of money growing exponentially over time based on the rate of interest and the number of compounding periods.

Defining Compound Interest

Compound interest is the interest calculated on both the principal amount and any previously earned interest. It allows your money to grow at an accelerated rate over time. The formula for compound interest is:

\(A=P(1+\frac{r}{n})^{nt}\)

Where:

  • \(A\) is the final amount (including principal and interest)
  • \(P\) is the principal amount (initial investment)
  • \(r\) is the annual interest rate (expressed as a decimal)
  • \(n\) is the number of times interest is compounded per year, aka the "frequency" of compounding. NOTICE that it shows up in two places in the equation
  • \(t\) is the time (in years) the money is invested for

Compound interest is a concept with remarkable potential for growing your wealth over time. Its power lies in its ability to generate exponential growth, fueled by the compounding effect. Unlike simple interest, which only accrues on the principal amount, compound interest takes into account both the principal and any previously earned interest.


Different Periods of Compounding Interest

The frequency of compounding (\(n\)) can significantly affect how much interest you earn. Common compounding frequencies include:

  • Annually (\(n=1\)): Interest is compounded once per year.
  • Semi-annually (\(n=2\)): Interest is compounded twice per year.
  • Quarterly (\(n=4\)): Interest is compounded four times per year.
  • Monthly (\(n=12\)): Interest is compounded twelve times per year.
  • Daily (\(n=365\)): Interest is compounded every day.

To illustrate, imagine you have $1,000 invested in an account with a 5% annual interest rate compounded annually. At the end of the first year, you would earn $50 in interest, bringing your total to $1,050. In the second year, the interest is calculated not only on the initial $1,000 but also on the $50 you earned in the first year. You might say that the old interest earned gets added to the principal amount. This compounding effect leads to accelerated growth. Let's delve deeper into the power of compounding and its impact on your investments.

As time passes, the accumulated interest is reinvested, which means that each subsequent period's interest is earned on a larger principal amount. This compounding effect allows your money to grow faster compared to simple interest. Over longer periods, the impact of compounding becomes more pronounced. The more time your money has to compound, the greater the growth potential.


Comparing Simple versus Compound Interest

To better understand the difference between simple and compound interest, let's examine a hypothetical scenario. Suppose you have $10,000 to invest for five years at a fixed interest rate of 6%. If the interest is compounded annually, the compound interest formula would be used to calculate the final amount:

  • \(A=P(1+\frac{r}{n})^{nt}\)
  • \(A=10000(1+\frac{0.06}{1})^{1\cdot 5}\)
  • \(A=10000(1.06)^5\)
  • \(A\approx $13,382.26\)

Now, let's compare this to simple interest. Since we are looking at total balances, let us use the second version of the simple interest formula, the interest earned would be:

  • \(B=(P\cdot r\cdot t)+P\)
  • \(B=(10000\cdot 0.06\cdot 5)+10000\)
  • \(B=3000+10000\)
  • \(B=$13,000\)

By comparing the results, we can see that compound interest offers a significant advantage over simple interest. In this example, compound interest generated $382.26 more in earnings compared to simple interest. Example 1

The value of an investment grows by 5% annually. If the initial investment is $1,000, write the exponential function equation representing this scenario.

We are told that the initial investment is 1000. So P = 1000.

Write an equation to represent this situation.

Solution:

We are told the investment grows by 5% annually. So the growth rate = 0.05.

The interest is being compounded annually which is a term of 1 time per year. Therefore, n=1.

So, the growth factor is \(1+\frac{0.05}{1}\).

Finally, we substitute these values for their respective placeholders and get the equation \(A=1000(1+0.05)^t\)


Example 2

You deposit $4,250 into a savings account with a 2.75% annual interest rate, compounded monthly. Write an equation that will calculate the amount in the savings account after t years.

Solution:

  • The initial amount invested is 4250. So, P=4250.
  • The interest rate is 2.75% or 0.0275 as a decimal.
  • It is compounded monthly which is a time period of 12 times a year. So, n=12.
  • The equation is \(A=4250(1+\frac{0.0275}{12})^{12t}\)


Conclusion

Compound interest is an essential concept in finance, illustrating how investments grow over time. Its relationship with exponential functions helps us understand and predict the growth of investments or debts. By mastering the compound interest formula, students can apply these principles to real-world financial decisions, understanding the impact of different compounding frequencies on their investments or loans.
