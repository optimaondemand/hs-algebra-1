💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In this lesson, we'll delve into what exponential growth functions are, how to identify them through their graphs, and how to determine if an equation represents exponential growth. Exponential growth functions are a crucial concept in Algebra 1, modeling situations where quantities increase rapidly over time.


What is an Exponential Growth Function?

An exponential growth function is a type of function where the rate of change increases exponentially, meaning the quantity grows at a rate proportional to its current value. These functions are commonly represented by the equation \(y=ab^x\), where:

  • \(y\) is the final amount,
  • \(a\) is the initial amount (when \(x=0\)),
  • \(b\) is the base of the exponential function, indicating the growth factor per unit increase in \(x\),
  • \(x\) rrepresents time or another independent variable.

For a function to model exponential growth, the base \(b\) must be greater than 1 (\(b>1\)). This condition ensures that as x increases, y increases at an accelerating rate. Described as end behavior, this means that as \(x\) approaches \(\infty\), then \(y\) approaches \(\infty\).

In general, an exponential function can be written as \(y=ab^x\), where \(y\) is the output, \(a\) is the initial value, \(b\) is the growth factor, and \(x\) is the input variable (often time).

Simply, an exponential function is a function that grows or decays at a constant percentage rate, that is by a common factor: for each unit change in \(x\) there is a corresponding change in \(y\) by a factor of \(b\) (where "factor" means "multiply"). Compare this to a linear function (\(y=mx+b\)) that increases or decreases by a common difference: for each unit change in \(x\), there is a corresponding change in \(y\) by a difference of m (where "difference" means "subtract").

For example, if an investment of \($2\) is growing at a rate of \(10%\) per year, it can be described using an exponential function: \(f(x)=2(1.1)^x\). For every increase in \(x\) by \(1\), the y-value increases by a factor of \(1.1\). But for a comparable linear function \(g(x)=1.1x+2\), each unit increase in \(x\), \(y\) increases by \(1.1\) units.

Determining Exponential Growth from Equations

When given an equation, you can determine if it represents exponential growth by examining its form and coefficients:

  1. Equation Form: Ensure the equation is in the form \(y=a(b)^x\).
  2. Base b: Check if the base \(b\) of the exponential function is greater than 1 (\(b>1\)). This condition is necessary for the function to model exponential growth.
  3. Initial Amount a: The initial amount \(a\) should be positive to indicate that the quantity starts above zero and grows from there.


Example 1

Consider the exponential function \(y=2(3)^x\). Does this function represent exponential growth?

Solution:

To find the base, we look at the value next to the exponent. In this case, the base is \(3\).

The initial value, "\(a\)," is \(2\), which represents the starting point of the function. That is, \(3^x=3^0=1\). Continuing, \(2\cdot 1=2\). So when \(x=0\) when \(y=2\).

Since the base is greater than 1 (\(3>1\)), the function exhibits exponential growth—for each unit increase in x (0, 1, 2, ... ) y increases by a factor of 3 (2, 6, 18, ... ).

This function represents exponential growth because as \(x\) increases, \(y\) increases at an accelerating rate due to the base \(b\) being greater than 1.

Now, let's summarize the key features of the exponential function \(y=2(3)^x\):

  • Base (b): 3
  • Initial Value (a): 2
  • Growth or Decay: Exponential growth



Identifying Exponential Growth on Graphs

To determine if a graph of an exponential function represents exponential growth, look for these key characteristics:

  1. Upward Slope: The graph slopes upwards from left to right, indicating an increase in y as x increases.
  2. Increasing Rate of Change: The graph should show an accelerating rate of increase, meaning the distance between the points on the graph grows as you move from left to right.
  3. Y-Intercept: The graph will cross the y-axis at \(y=a\), where a is the initial amount. This point should be positive (\(a>0\)) for exponential growth.
  4. Asymptote: The graph has a horizontal asymptote, typically along the x-axis (\(y-0\)), which the graph approaches but never touches as x decreases.

Conclusion

Understanding exponential growth functions is vital for modeling and interpreting scenarios where quantities increase rapidly over time, such as population growth or compound interest. By examining the graph's shape and the equation's structure, you can identify exponential growth functions and predict their behavior. This knowledge lays the groundwork for further exploration of exponential functions and their applications in various fields.
