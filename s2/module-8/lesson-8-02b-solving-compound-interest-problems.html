📚 VOCABULARY

Annual Percentage Rate (APR): The annualized interest rate charged on a loan or investment, expressed as a percentage. It represents the cost of borrowing or the potential return on investment, excluding compounding effects.

Annual percentage yield (APY): The effective interest rate that takes into account the compounding periods per year, providing a more accurate measure of the true return on an investment. See also Effective Annual Rate.

Compound interest: The type of interest that is calculated on the initial principal as well as the accumulated interest from previous periods, resulting in exponential growth.

Compound interest formula: The mathematical equation used to calculate the future value of an investment or loan that takes into account the principal, interest rate, compounding periods, and time period.

Compounding periods: The number of times interest is compounded per unit of time, such as annually, semi-annually, quarterly, or monthly. See also 'Frequency'.

Effective Annual Rate (EAR): The effective interest rate that takes into account the compounding periods per year, providing a more accurate measure of the true return on an investment. See also Annual Percentage Yield.

Exponential growth: A type of growth in which the quantity or value increases rapidly over time, following an exponential function.

Final amount: The total value of an investment or loan, including the principal and accumulated compound interest, at the end of a specified time period.

Financial context: The specific financial situation or scenario in which concepts, calculations, or decisions related to money and finance are applied.

Fixed deposit account: A type of investment account offered by banks or financial institutions where a fixed amount of money is deposited for a specific time period at a predetermined interest rate.

Frequency : The number of compounding periods within a given time frame. It determines how often the interest is calculated and added to the principal. Common frequencies include annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, or daily. See also 'Compounding periods'.

Future value: The total amount of money, including principal and accumulated interest, that an investment will grow to after a specified time period.

Interest rate: The percentage or decimal value that represents the cost of borrowing money or the return on an investment, per unit of time.

Principal: The initial amount of money on which simple interest is calculated or the original amount borrowed or invested.

Simple interest: The amount of money earned or paid on a loan or investment that is calculated based on the initial amount (principal), the interest rate, and the time period.

Time period: The length of time for which interest is calculated or the duration of a loan or investment, usually measured in years.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore solving compound interest problems. Some of them are straightforward calculations, like determining how much you will have paid on a loan after 7 years. Others will be comparing the compound interest rates on investments offered by competing banks. First, we will review what compound interest is and how to calculate it. Then we will look at another way to compare rates.

 

Compound interest is when you earn interest on both the initial amount of money you have and the interest that you've already earned. And then earn interest on all of that. And then earn interest on all of that. In short, it's a way for your money (or your debts!) to grow faster over time.

The formula to calculate the compound interest is: \(A=P(1+\frac{r}{n})^{nt}\).

To solve compound interest problems, we need to know a few key pieces of information: the principal (or initial amount of money, P), the interest rate (written as a decimal, r), the time period (measured in years, t),  and the number of times the interest is compounded per year (once, twice, quarterly, monthly, daily, or even continuously, though we will not explore continuous compound interest at this level). 'A' is the total account balance, principal plus all accrued interest.


Straightforward Calculations

Let's say you have $1,000 as your principal amount, and the interest rate is 5% per year. The time period is 3 years, and the interest is compounded annually (once per year). Let's substitute the values into the formula and solve the problem step by step.

Solution:

  1. Convert the interest rate to decimal form. In this case, 5% becomes 0.05.
  2. Substitute all known values into the formula: \(A=1000(1+\frac{0.05}{1})^{1\cdot 3}\)
  3. Simplify inside both sets of parentheses: \(A=1000(1.05)^3\)
  4. Evaluate the expression inside the parentheses raised to its relevant power: \(A=1000(1.157625)\)
  5. Multiply to find the total amount: \(A\approx $1,157.63\)

(Depending on whether you are using a calculator and what kind, you might simply evaluate the entire expression to solve for A after substituting in all known values.)

So, after 3 years, the total amount of money you'll have is approximately $1,157.63.


Example 1

First, let's say you invest $1,000 at an annual interest rate of 4.3% compounded quarterly for 4 years.

How much would you have in the account after 4 years?

Solution:

To find the final amount, substitute the following values into the compound interest formula:

  • P = 1000 (initial investment)
  • r = 4.3% = 0.043 (interest rate)
  • n = 4 (compounded quarterly, that is, four times per year)
  • t = 4 years

So, the formula looks like this:

  • \(A=P(1+\frac{r}{n})^{nt}\)
  • \(A=1000(1+\frac{0.043}{4})^{(4\cdot 4)}\)
  • \(A=1000(1.01075)^{16}\) - It would be best at this point to plug that whole expression into your calculator at once to get the answer in the final bullet.
  • \(A=1000(1.186588131...)\)
  • \(A\approx $1,186.59\)


Example 2

The frequency of compounding plays a crucial role in determining the final amount. Generally, compounding can occur annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, or even daily, depending on the financial institution or investment product. The more frequently interest is compounded within a year, the greater the impact on the total growth of your investment.

Solution:

For instance, let's consider a $10,000 investment with a 6% annual interest rate compounded annually, semi-annually, and monthly, all for five years:

Annually compounded:

  • \(A=10000(1+\frac{0.06}{1})^{(1\cdot 5)}\)
  • \(A=10000(1.06)^5\) - Plug into calculator
  • \(A\approx $13,382.26\)


Semi-annually compounded:

  • \(A=10000(1+\frac{0.06}{2})^{(2\cdot 5)}\)
  • \(A=10000(1.03)^{10}\)
  • \(A\approx $13,439.16\)


Monthly compounded:

  • \(A=10000(1+\frac{0.06}{12})^{(12\cdot 5)}\)
  • \(A=10000(1.005)^{60}\)
  • \(A\approx $13,488.50\)

By comparing the final amounts, we can observe that the more frequent the compounding, the higher the final value. While in this example the difference is not a substantial amount (not even $107 for the year) recall that with compound interest every little bit helps. Even seemingly small differences in compounding frequency can have a substantial impact on the growth of your investment over time.


Compound interest problems can vary in complexity, but the basic steps remain the same. Just make sure to substitute the given values into the formula correctly, perform the necessary calculations step by step, and you'll be able to solve compound interest problems.
