📚 VOCABULARY

Coefficient: A numerical or constant quantity placed before and multiplying the variable in an algebraic term. For example; in the term \(4x\), 4 is the coefficient.

Completing the Square: A method used to solve quadratic equations by adding a term to both sides of the equation so that one side forms a perfect square trinomial.

Constant Term: A term in an algebraic expression or equation that does not contain any variables.

Difference of Squares: A term that can be expressed in the form \(a^2-b^2\), which factors into \((a+b)(a-b)\).

Factored Form: An expression presented as a product of its factors. For quadratic equations, this form is \(y=a(x-p)(x-q)\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are the roots of the equation.

Leading Coefficient: The coefficient of the term with the highest degree in a polynomial. For example, in the quadratic equation \(y=3x^2-5x+6\), \(3\) is the leading coefficient.

Maximum/Minimum: The highest or lowest point on the graph of a quadratic function, respectively. For a parabola, this point is at the vertex.

Parabola: The shape of the graph of a quadratic function.

Perfect Square: A term or expression that is the square of a polynomial. For example, \(x^2+2x+1=(x+1)^2\).

Quadratic Equation: An equation of the form \(y=ax^2+bx+c\).

Solution: The value(s) of the variable that satisfy an equation, inequality, or system of equations.

Square Root: A value that, when multipled by itself, goves the original number. For example, the square root of \(9\) is \(3\) because \(3^2=9\).

Standard Form: A way of writing down equations so that all terms are aligned on one side. The standard form of a quadratic equation is \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are real numbers.

Vertex: The highest or lowest point of a parabola, depending on whether the parabola opens upwards or downwards. It is the point \((h,k)\) in the vertex form of a quadratic function, \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\).

Vertex Form: A form of a quadratic equation that makes it easy to find the vertex. It is written as \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), where \((h,k)\) is the vertex.

x-intercept: The point(s) at which the graph intersects the x-axis. At these points, the value of y is 0.

Zero Product Property: A principle stating that if a product of two or more factors is zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero.

Zeros: The value(s) of x that make the equation equal to zero. They are the roots or solutions of the equation and are the x-intercepts of the graph of the equation.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore what the zeros of a quadratic function are and how to determine the zeros from a graph or an equation. We will explore finding the zeros from an equation when the equations are given in factored form and standard form. It is important to understand what the graph of a quadratic function tells us and how that is connected to the different forms of equations that quadratic functions can be presented in.


Let's begin by understanding what the zeros of a quadratic function are and watch this short video on how to find the zeros from a graph representing a quadratic function.

Understanding the Zeros of a Quadratic Function

In algebra, a quadratic function is often represented by the equation \(y=ax^2+bx+c\), where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are constants. The graph of a quadratic function is a parabola, which can open upwards when \(a>0\) or downwards when \(a<0\). A key feature of this graph is its zeros, also known as roots or x-intercepts. These are the points where the parabola crosses the x-axis, meaning the value of the output \(y\) is zero.

Quadratic functions can have:

  • 2 zeros - when it crosses the x-axis twice.
  • 1 zero - when its vertex is on the x-axis.
  • 0 zeros - when it is above the x-axis and opening upward or below and opening downward.

How to Determine Zeros from a Graph

To find the zeros of a quadratic function from its graph, simply look for the points where the parabola intersects the x-axis. These intersection points give you the x-values for which \(y=0\).


Example 1

The graph shown represents a quadratic function with the equation \(y=-\frac{2}{7}(x+7)(x-6)\).

What are the zeros of the quadratic function shown in the graph?

Solution:

  • The zeros are the points where the graph crosses the x-axis.
  • We notice that the graph crosses the x-axis in 2 places: \(-7\) and \(6\)
  • To write the answers that represent the zeros, we must write them as ordered pairs since they represent points.
    • The left zero is \((-7,0)\).
    • The right zero is \((6,0)\).

The zeros of the quadratic function are \(\{(-7,0),(6,0)\}\).


Now, let's explore finding the zeros of a quadratic function when given only the equation of the quadratic function. We will explore to forms of the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • Determining Zeros from an Equation in Factored Form
    • A quadratic equation in factored form looks like \(y=a(x-p)(x-q)\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are the zeros of the function.
  • Determining Zeros from an Equation in Standard Form
    • A quadratic in standard form looks like \(y=ax^2+bx+c\), where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are constants.
    • The zeros can be found by using the quadratic formula or by factoring.

Determining Zeros from an Equation

Example 2

The equation \(y=3(x+11)(x+3)\) represents a quadratic function.

Find the zeros of the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • Since the equation is in factored form, we will use the zero product property. This was explored in an earlier lesson in module 5 - solving one-variable quadratic equations.
  • We begin by substituting in 0 for y which will set the equation equal to 0.
    • \(0=3(x+11)(x+3)\)
  • Then we can divide both sides by 3 to isolate our factors. Since 0 divided by anything is still 0, it won't change the equation much.
    • \(0=(x+11)(x+3)\)
  • Now, we separate our factors and set each of them equal to zero to solve.
    • \(0-11=x+11-11\) or \(0-3=x+3-3\)
    • \(-11=x\) or \(-3=x\)
  • We find that \(x=-11\) or \(x=-3\)
    • Notice that these values are the opposite of the values inside the parenthesis. It will always work this way.
    • Don't forget to write them as ordered pairs to represent them as the POINTS where the graph crosses the x-axis.

The zeros of the quadratic function are \(\{(-11,0),(-3,0)\}\).


To find the zeros when the equation is in standard form, we have two options.

  • Use the quadratic formula -  when \(y=ax^2+bx+c\).
  • Factor the equation using factoring techniques from Module 5.

Let's look at an example for both techniques.


Example 3

The equation \(y=x^2-x-20\) represents a quadratic function.

Find the zeros of the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • You might notice that the function is easily factorable.
    • To factor, we look for what multiplies to make \(-20\) and will add to make \(-1\).
    • We find that \(-5\cdot4=-20\) and \(-5+4=-1\)
  • The quadratic equation can be rewritten into factored form using the factors \(-5\) and \(4\).
    • \(y=(x-5)(x+4)\)
  • Now, we can use what we learned in the previous example by setting it equal to zero and using the zero product property.
    • \(0=(x+5)(x+4)\)
    • \(0=x-5\) or \(0=x+4\)
    • \(0+5=x-5+5 \implies 5=x\) or \(0-4=x+4-4 \implies -4=x\)
    • \(x=5\) or \(x=-4\)
  • We find that the zeros are \((5,0)\) and \((-4,0)\)

The zeros of the quadratic function are \(\{(5,0),(-4,0)\}\).


Example 4

The equation \(y=10x^2+13x-3\) represents a quadratic function.

Find the zeros of the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • This equation is not as easily factorable as the previous example. So, we can use the quadratic formula.
    • The equation may be factorable and if you are good with factoring, you can always use that method if it is factorable.
  • From the equation, we know that a=10, b=13, and c=-3
  • We substitute these values into the quadratic formula.
    • Now, we evaluate the quadratic formula.
  • We find that the zeros are \((\frac{1}{5},0)\) and \((-\frac{3}{2},0)\).

The zeros of the quadratic function are \(\{(\frac{1}{5},0),(-\frac{3}{2},0)\}\).


Conclusion

The zeros of a quadratic function are crucial for understanding its graph. They indicate where the parabola crosses the x-axis. By analyzing the graph, using the factored form, or applying the quadratic formula to the standard form, students can identify these zeros. Whether the parabola opens upwards or downwards, and regardless of the signs of the zeros, these methods provide a consistent approach to finding the roots of the quadratic function. This lesson underscores the interconnectedness between algebraic expressions and their graphical representations, enhancing comprehension of quadratic functions and their properties. the Zero Product Property allows us to find the values of x that make the function equal zero, revealing the points where the graph intersects the x-axis. This method not only helps in solving quadratic equations but also in understanding the function's graph.
