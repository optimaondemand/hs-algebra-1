📚 VOCABULARY

Coefficient: A numerical or constant quantity placed before and multiplying the variable in an algebraic term. For example; in the term \(4x\), 4 is the coefficient.

Completing the Square: A method used to solve quadratic equations by adding a term to both sides of the equation so that one side forms a perfect square trinomial.

Constant Term: A term in an algebraic expression or equation that does not contain any variables.

Difference of Squares: A term that can be expressed in the form \(a^2-b^2\), which factors into \((a+b)(a-b)\).

Factored Form: An expression presented as a product of its factors. For quadratic equations, this form is \(y=a(x-p)(x-q)\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are the roots of the equation.

Leading Coefficient: The coefficient of the term with the highest degree in a polynomial. For example, in the quadratic equation \(y=3x^2-5x+6\), \(3\) is the leading coefficient.

Maximum/Minimum: The highest or lowest point on the graph of a quadratic function, respectively. For a parabola, this point is at the vertex.

Parabola: The shape of the graph of a quadratic function.

Perfect Square: A term or expression that is the square of a polynomial. For example, \(x^2+2x+1=(x+1)^2\).

Quadratic Equation: An equation of the form \(y=ax^2+bx+c\).

Solution: The value(s) of the variable that satisfy an equation, inequality, or system of equations.

Square Root: A value that, when multipled by itself, goves the original number. For example, the square root of \(9\) is \(3\) because \(3^2=9\).

Standard Form: A way of writing down equations so that all terms are aligned on one side. The standard form of a quadratic equation is \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are real numbers.

Vertex: The highest or lowest point of a parabola, depending on whether the parabola opens upwards or downwards. It is the point \((h,k)\) in the vertex form of a quadratic function, \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\).

Vertex Form: A form of a quadratic equation that makes it easy to find the vertex. It is written as \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), where \((h,k)\) is the vertex.

x-intercept: The point(s) at which the graph intersects the x-axis. At these points, the value of y is 0.

Zero Product Property: A principle stating that if a product of two or more factors is zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero.

Zeros: The value(s) of x that make the equation equal to zero. They are the roots or solutions of the equation and are the x-intercepts of the graph of the equation.

We will look at how to determine the vertex of a quadratic function when given the graph or the equation representing a quadratic function. We will explore quadratic equations that are given in vertex form and standard form. We will understand how the vertex relates the quadratic equation and the graph and what it means for the quadratic function.

Let's begin by learning what a vertex is. Then we will watch a video on determining the vertex of a quadratic function when given the graph.


What is the Vertex?

In the context of quadratic functions, the vertex is a point on the graph that represents the highest or lowest value, depending on the orientation of the parabola. If the parabola is opening upward, then the vertex will be the lowest point at the bottom of the parabola. If the parabola is opening downwards, then the vertex will be the highest point at the top of the parabola.

(myrec)

  • The vertex of the quadratic function shown in the graph is the highest point of the parabola since the parabola is opening downwards. We notice that this point is marked.
  • To identify the vertex, we need to find the coordinates representing this point. Starting at (0,0), we will go to the right 1 and down 5 units to get to the point representing the vertex.
  • Vertex = (1, -5)


Now, let's look at how to determine the vertex of a quadratic function when given just the equation. This will help us understand how the graph and the equation of a quadratic function is related.

Vertex Form of a Quadratic Equation

The vertex form of a quadratic equation is given by:

\(y=a(x-h)^2+k\)

In this form, finding the vertex is straightforward because the equation directly gives you the vertex (h, k). Here, h and k represent the x and y coordinates of the vertex, respectively. The coefficient of a determines whether the parabola opens upwards or downwards, but doesn't affect the vertex's location.


Example 2

The given equation represents a quadratic function.

\(y=2(x-5)^2+7\)

Identify the coordinates of the vertex for the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • The value of h is 5 and the value of k is 7.
  • The vertex is \((5,7)\).


Example 3

The given equation represents a quadratic function.

\(y=-\frac{2}{3}(x+11)^2-9\)

Identify the coordinates of the vertex for the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • The value of h is -11 and the value of k is -9.
  • The vertex is \((-11,-9)\).


**Note that we use the opposite sign in front of the h from the equation. When is subtraction, we used a positive value and when it was adding, we used a negative value. We used the same sign of the k value. When it was adding, we used a positive and when it was subtraction we used a negative value.

We can also identify if the vertex will be a maximum or a minimum based on which way the parabola opens. We can identify if the parabola opens upwards when a is positive and the parabola opens downwards when a is negative.

In the first example, the vertex is a minimum since \(a=2\) which is positive. The parabola opens upwards and the vertex is at the bottom.

In the second example, the vertex is a maximum since \(a=-\frac{2}{3}\) which is negative. The parabola opens downwards and the vertex is at the top.


Last, we are going to look at how to determine the vertex of a quadratic function when given an equation in standard form. There are two ways we can go about this and we will explore both methods.

Let's begin with the first method and usually the most straightforward method no matter what the coefficients and constants are.


Standard Form of a Quadratic

\(y=ax^2+bx+c\)

where a, b, and c are real numbers.

When a quadratic is in standard form, we can use the formula for the axis of symmetry which is also the x-coordinate of the vertex. Then we substitute the value into the original equation to find the y-value. This method works even if the values of a, b, and c are not so clean.

Steps to Find the Vertex in Standard Form:

  1. Calculate the x-coordinate of the vertex using \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\)​.
  2. Substitute the x-coordinate back into the original equation to find the y-coordinate.

Let's look at an example using this method.


Example 4

The given equation represents a quadratic function.

\(y=3x^2-15x+7\)

Identify the coordinates of the vertex for the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • Let's begin with using the formula to find the x-coordinate of the vertex.
    • We will use the values of a and b from the equation. \(a=3\) and \(b=-15\)
    • \(x=-\frac{-15}{2\cdot3}=\frac{15}{6}=\frac{5}{2}=2.5\)
  • Now, we substitute the x value into the original equation to find the value of y.
    • \(y=3(2.5)^2-15(2.5)+7\)
    • \(y=3(6.25)-15(2.5)+7\)
    • \(y=18.75-37.5+7\)
    • \(y=-11.75\)
  • We find that the vertex of the quardratic function is \(V=(2.5,-11.75)\)


The other method we can use to find the vertex is to complete the square with the equation to rearrange the equation into vertex form and follow what we did in the first example.

Steps to Complete the Square

Completing the square involves adding and subtracting a certain value to make a trinomial a perfect square trinomial, which can then be factored into a binomial squared. Here's how to do it:

1. Start with the Standard Form

2. Factor out 'a' if there is a coefficient on the \(x^2\).

3. Separate the first two terms from the constant using brackets

4. Find the perfect square using the bx term that will complete the square.

To find the squared term to complete the square use the formula \((\frac{b}{2})^2\).

5. Add the square inside the brack and subtract the square outside the brackets to keep the equation balanced.

6. Factor the perfect square trinomial inside the brackets and combine the two constants at the end.

This will give you the vertex form of a quadratic equation.


Let's look at how to complete the square for a quadratic equation in standard form to put it into vertex form.

Example 5

The given equation represents a quadratic function.

\(y=x^2-6x+11\)

Identify the coordinates of the vertex for the quadratic function.

Solution:

  • We begin by separating the first two terms from the constant
  • Next, we take half of b and square it to find the perfect square.
    • \((\frac{-6}{2})^2=(-3)^2=9\)
  • We will add this perfect square inside the brackets to make the perfect square trinomial. Then subtract it outside to keep the equation balanced.
    • \(y=[x^2-6x+\boxed{9}]+11-\boxed{9}\)
  • Now, we factor the perfect square trinomial inside the brackets and combine the constants outside the brackets.
    • \(y=(x-3)^2+2\)
    • The perfect square trinomial will always factor as a binomial squared.

The vertex form of the original equation is \(y=(x-3)^2+2\),

We find that the vertex is \(V=(3,2)\).


This method will work on most quadratics in standard form. It can become ugly if you have to factor out an a value that isn't a perfect factor or if the value of b is not an even number. Then you start dealing with fractions. Therefore, the first method we explored using the equation \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\) would be a more preferred method.


Conclusion

Today, we've learned about the importance of the vertex in understanding quadratic functions. By being able to identify and analyze the vertex, you gain deeper insights into the nature of quadratic functions and how they graphically represent mathematical relationships. Understanding how to find the vertex of a quadratic function, whether from its vertex form or standard form, is a fundamental skill in Algebra 1. It allows you to graph the function accurately, understand its properties, and solve a variety of practical problems.
